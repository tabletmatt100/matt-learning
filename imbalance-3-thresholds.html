Threshold moving and cost-based decisions

TL;DR

  • Your classifier outputs scores. The 0.5 cutoff that turns them into labels is a default, and on a 1% problem a calibrated model will put almost nothing above it.
  • With costs per mistake, flag when $p > C_{FP}/(C_{FP}+C_{FN})$. Review at 5 dollars, missed fraud at 200 dollars: flag above 0.024, not 0.5.
  • On the simulated fraud model below, that one change cuts total cost from about 14,500 to about 6,400 per 10,000 transactions. Same model, no retraining.
  • No costs? Pick the threshold from a review budget or a recall target. The best-F1 threshold is a different number again, and F1 knows nothing about what errors cost.
  • When the loss depends on the transaction amount, the rule becomes "flag if $p \times \text{amount}$ is more than the review cost".
  • Choose the threshold on validation data or out-of-fold predictions. Never on the test set.

1. Scores, not labels: why 0.5 fails at 1%

The running example in this series: a card-fraud model scored on 10,000 transactions, of which 100 are fraud (a 1% base rate, 99:1). At its current cutoff it produces the confusion matrix TP = 80, FN = 20, FP = 320, TN = 9,580. Part 1 covers where those numbers come from and why accuracy hides the problem.

Here's the thing a lot of tutorials skip: most classifiers don't output classes, they output scores. The 0.5 cutoff that turns a score into a label is a default somebody picked, not a law. In scikit-learn, calling predict() instead of predict_proba() quietly applies that 0.5 for you. On a 1% problem, a perfectly calibrated model will rarely put any transaction above 0.5, because even a suspicious transaction is usually more likely legit than fraud. So at 0.5 it predicts "legit" almost always, and people conclude the model "can't learn the minority class". It often learned fine. The cutoff is just wrong.

The Bayes arithmetic behind it

Bayes' rule in odds form makes this obvious. Start from the prior odds, multiply by how much more likely the evidence is under fraud than under legit (the likelihood ratio), and you get the posterior odds:

$$\underbrace{\frac{P(\text{fraud}\mid x)}{P(\text{legit}\mid x)}}_{\text{posterior odds}} = \underbrace{\frac{100}{9{,}900}}_{\text{prior odds} \,=\, 1/99} \times \underbrace{\frac{p(x\mid\text{fraud})}{p(x\mid\text{legit})}}_{\text{likelihood ratio}}$$

A score above 0.5 means posterior odds above 1. With prior odds of 1/99, that needs a likelihood ratio above 99. The evidence has to be 99 times more typical of fraud than of legit traffic just to reach a coin flip.

In the simulated model used in the widgets, legit $x \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and fraud $x \sim \mathcal{N}(2.5,1)$, so the likelihood ratio is $e^{2.5x - 3.125}$. Setting it to 99:

$$2.5x - 3.125 = \ln 99 = 4.595 \;\Rightarrow\; x = \frac{4.595 + 3.125}{2.5} = 3.088$$

Fraud cases have mean 2.5, so only those above $3.088$, that is $1 - \Phi(0.588) = 0.278$ of them, get a score over 0.5. That's about 28 of 100. Legit cases above 3.088: $9{,}900 \times (1 - \Phi(3.088)) \approx 10$. So only 38 of 10,000 transactions (0.38%) ever cross 0.5, even though the model ranks fraud well.

Same story with the running confusion matrix. The model flags 80 + 320 = 400 transactions and 80 of them are fraud. If its scores were calibrated, those 400 scores would average $80/400 = 0.2$. A typical flagged case is a one-in-five fraud risk, which is well worth a 5-dollar look and nowhere near 0.5.

In practice, I'd try threshold moving first, before any resampling or reweighting. It costs nothing, doesn't change the model, and doesn't distort probabilities. Pick the threshold on a validation set (never on test; section 7 says why).

2. Picking the threshold from costs

If you can put rough costs on mistakes, the decision becomes simple. Flag a case when the expected cost of not flagging exceeds the expected cost of flagging. With calibrated probability $p$ of fraud:

$$p \cdot C_{FN} > (1-p) \cdot C_{FP} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p > t^* = \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP} + C_{FN}}$$

Say a manual review costs about 5 dollars of analyst time ($C_{FP} = 5$), and a missed fraud costs about 200 dollars in chargebacks ($C_{FN} = 200$).

$$t^* = \frac{5}{5 + 200} = \frac{5}{205} \approx 0.024$$

So you should flag anything with more than a 2.4% chance of fraud. That's twenty times lower than the default. If the costs were equal, you'd get $t^* = 0.5$, which is the only situation where the default makes sense.

Check it at the boundary. At $p = 0.024$: not flagging costs $0.024 \times 200 = 4.88$ in expectation; flagging costs $0.976 \times 5 = 4.88$. Equal, as it should be. At $p = 0.05$: not flagging costs $10.00$, flagging costs $4.75$, so flag.

You can also score a whole confusion matrix this way. The running example at $C_{FP} = 5$, $C_{FN} = 200$ costs $320 \times 5 + 20 \times 200 = 1{,}600 + 4{,}000 = 5{,}600$ dollars. Compare the two lazy extremes: flag nothing and you eat all 100 frauds, $100 \times 200 = 20{,}000$; flag everything and you review all 9,900 legit transactions, $9{,}900 \times 5 = 49{,}500$.

This assumes the scores are calibrated probabilities (Part 6 covers how to get them back). If they aren't, the formula gives the wrong number, but you can still sweep thresholds on validation data and pick the one with lowest total cost. The idea is old: Elkan (2001) laid out this threshold and its link to reweighting, which is why class weights and threshold moving are two routes to the same decision.

Derivation: expected cost with a full cost matrix

Write a cost matrix with one cost per cell: $C_{TP}, C_{FN}, C_{FP}, C_{TN}$. For a case with fraud probability $p$:

$$E[\text{cost} \mid \text{flag}] = p\,C_{TP} + (1-p)\,C_{FP}$$ $$E[\text{cost} \mid \text{pass}] = p\,C_{FN} + (1-p)\,C_{TN}$$

Flag when the first is smaller. Rearranging:

$$p\,(C_{FN} - C_{TP}) > (1-p)\,(C_{FP} - C_{TN}) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; p > t^* = \frac{C_{FP} - C_{TN}}{(C_{FP} - C_{TN}) + (C_{FN} - C_{TP})}$$

Only the differences matter: the regret of a false alarm, $C_{FP} - C_{TN}$, and the regret of a miss, $C_{FN} - C_{TP}$. The short formula in the main text is the special case $C_{TP} = C_{TN} = 0$.

That special case quietly assumes reviewing a real fraud is free. If every review costs 5 dollars, fraud or not, then $C_{TP} = 5$ and

$$t^* = \frac{5 - 0}{(5 - 0) + (200 - 5)} = \frac{5}{200} = 0.025$$

A tiny change here, but with cheaper misses relative to reviews the gap grows. Summed over a data set, total cost is $C_{TP}\cdot TP + C_{FN}\cdot FN + C_{FP}\cdot FP + C_{TN}\cdot TN$, which is what the threshold sweep minimises.

4. Best F1 is not minimum cost

Press "Best F1" in the explorer. With the defaults it lands near $t = 0.24$, F1 0.479, flagging about 87 cases and catching about 45. Total cost there is about 11,200, nearly double the 6,393 at $t^*$. F1 doesn't know what your errors cost.

F1 treats a missed fraud and a false alarm as roughly comparable, and it ignores true negatives completely. When a miss is 40 times worse than a false alarm, that puts the F1 threshold far too high. When a false alarm is the expensive one (say each flag freezes a customer's card and they leave), it could put it too low. People reach for F1 because it needs no cost numbers, which is fair, but then you should know it is making a cost assumption for you.

There's a neat result for calibrated scores, from Lipton, Elkan and Naryanaswamy (2014): the F1-optimal threshold is half the best achievable F1. Check it on the explorer: best F1 0.479, half of that is 0.240, and the grid found 0.241. One consequence: a model that can only reach F1 0.2 should be thresholded at about 0.1, which is far below what most people would guess.

A calibrated churn model's best achievable F1 on validation is 0.48. Without running a sweep, where would you expect the F1-maximising threshold to be?

5. Review budgets and recall targets

Often nobody will give you a cost for a missed fraud. What you get instead is a constraint. Two common ones:

  • A review budget: "analysts can handle 500 cases a day". Pick $t$ so the expected flagged count equals 500. If you see 10,000 transactions a day, that's the top 5% of scores.
  • A recall target: "we must catch 90% of fraud". Pick the highest $t$ that still has recall of at least 0.9 on validation data.

On the simulated model ($d = 2.5$), a budget of 500 per 10,000 needs $t \approx 0.032$. That flags 78.2 frauds and 421.8 legit: precision $78.2/500 = 0.156$, recall 0.782, cost $5 \times 421.8 + 200 \times 21.8 = 2{,}109 + 4{,}360 \approx 6{,}470$.

That is only about 77 dollars more than the unconstrained optimum (6,393, which flags about 610). The cost curve is flat near its minimum, so a modest capacity limit hardly hurts.

A 90% recall target is a different story: $t \approx 0.0092$, about 1,194 flagged, of which 1,104 are false alarms. Precision $90/1{,}194 = 0.075$, cost $5 \times 1{,}104 + 200 \times 10 = 7{,}521$. Push to 95% recall and you flag about 2,038 for a cost near 10,700. The last few frauds are expensive to catch because they look like legit traffic.

A budget is a ceiling, not a target. If $t^*$ flags fewer cases than the analysts can handle, use $t^*$ and leave the spare capacity. Filling the queue just because there's room raises cost. The widget below makes that call for you.

6. When the cost depends on the transaction

A flat 200-dollar miss cost is a convenient lie. Missing fraud on a 12-dollar coffee isn't the same as missing it on a 2,000-dollar laptop. If a missed fraud loses the transaction amount $A$ and a review costs $c$ whatever happens, the comparison per transaction is:

$$\text{flag if}\quad p \times A > c \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p > \frac{c}{A}$$

(This is the full cost-matrix formula with $C_{TP} = C_{FP} = c$, $C_{FN} = A$, $C_{TN} = 0$: $t^* = c/(c + A - c) = c/A$.) Each transaction gets its own threshold. This is called example-dependent cost-sensitive classification, and card fraud is the textbook case.

Review cost $c = 5$.

A 40-dollar purchase with $p = 0.08$: expected loss if passed $0.08 \times 40 = 3.20$, below 5. Let it through. Its personal threshold is $5/40 = 0.125$.

A 2,000-dollar purchase with $p = 0.01$: expected loss $0.01 \times 2{,}000 = 20$, above 5. Review it. Its threshold is $5/2{,}000 = 0.0025$.

A flat $t^* = 0.024$ gets both of these backwards: it flags the coffee-sized one at 8% and passes the laptop at 1%.

Real fraud costs aren't quite this clean. Some losses are recovered, flags annoy good customers, and a declined legit purchase has its own cost. But even a rough amount-aware rule usually beats a flat cutoff, and it is just a different threshold on the same model. You don't need to retrain anything.

7. Tuning the threshold without fooling yourself

The threshold is a fitted parameter, the same as any weight in the model. Pick it on the test set and the test set stops being a test. With only 100 positives, the score at the best threshold is partly luck, and choosing the luckiest threshold then reporting its score bakes the luck in. That's optimistic bias.

I ran a quick simulation of the explorer's model ($d = 2.5$, 100 fraud in 10,000), repeated 200 times. Picking the best-F1 threshold on a sample and scoring F1 on that same sample gave 0.498 on average. The same thresholds on a fresh sample averaged 0.471. The true best possible F1 for this model is 0.479, so tuning on the evaluation data overstated it by about 3 points, more than the gap between many models people argue about.

Use a separate validation split, or better, out-of-fold predictions from stratified cross-validation, so the threshold is chosen on every training row without any row scoring itself. Then apply that fixed threshold to the test set once. Part 2 has the wider story on leakage.

Since scikit-learn 1.5 this is built in. TunedThresholdClassifierCV wraps a classifier, cross-validates the threshold for a scoring function you choose, and then predict() uses it. Its sibling FixedThresholdClassifier just sets a threshold you already know, such as $t^* = 0.024$ from costs. For a cost objective, write the cost as a function and wrap it with make_scorer.

Code: tuning the threshold on validation data 
import numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics import precision_recall_curve

# p_val: validation scores, y_val: validation labels
prec, rec, thr = precision_recall_curve(y_val, p_val)
f1 = 2 * prec * rec / np.clip(prec + rec, 1e-12, None)
best_f1_t = thr[np.argmax(f1[:-1])]   # last point has no threshold

# cost-based sweep (works even if scores aren't calibrated)
C_FP, C_FN = 5, 200
ts = np.linspace(0.001, 0.999, 999)
cost = [C_FP * ((p_val >= t) & (y_val == 0)).sum()
        + C_FN * ((p_val <  t) & (y_val == 1)).sum() for t in ts]
best_cost_t = ts[int(np.argmin(cost))]

# scikit-learn >= 1.5 can do this inside CV:
from sklearn.model_selection import TunedThresholdClassifierCV
tuned = TunedThresholdClassifierCV(base_clf, scoring="f1", cv=5).fit(X_train, y_train)
print(tuned.best_threshold_)

The precision_recall_curve call returns one more precision/recall value than thresholds, hence the [:-1].

Code: budget, recall target and cost scorer 
import numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics import precision_recall_curve, confusion_matrix, make_scorer
from sklearn.model_selection import TunedThresholdClassifierCV, cross_val_predict

# out-of-fold scores on the training data (stratified 5-fold by default for classifiers)
p_oof = cross_val_predict(base_clf, X_train, y_train, cv=5, method="predict_proba")[:, 1]

# review budget: 500 per 10,000 means flag the top 5% of scores
t_budget = np.quantile(p_oof, 1 - 500 / 10_000)

# recall target: highest threshold that still catches 90%
prec, rec, thr = precision_recall_curve(y_train, p_oof)
t_recall = thr[rec[:-1] >= 0.90].max()

# cost objective inside CV
def total_cost(y_true, y_pred, c_fp=5, c_fn=200):
    tn, fp, fn, tp = confusion_matrix(y_true, y_pred, labels=[0, 1]).ravel()
    return c_fp * fp + c_fn * fn

cost_scorer = make_scorer(total_cost, greater_is_better=False)
tuned = TunedThresholdClassifierCV(base_clf, scoring=cost_scorer, cv=5).fit(X_train, y_train)
print(tuned.best_threshold_, -tuned.best_score_)   # scorer negates costs

One more trap. A threshold chosen on calibrated scores assumes the production base rate matches the training one. If fraud doubles from 1% to 2% next quarter (prior shift), the model's scores understate the real risk, and the same threshold now flags too few cases. Budget-based thresholds drift too: the count above a fixed $t$ changes with traffic. Re-check the flagged volume and precision every week or so, and see Part 6 for correcting probabilities to a new prior.

Spot the bug in this evaluation:

clf.fit(X_train, y_train)
p_test = clf.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]
prec, rec, thr = precision_recall_curve(y_test, p_test)
f1 = 2 * prec * rec / np.clip(prec + rec, 1e-12, None)
t = thr[np.argmax(f1[:-1])]
print("test F1:", f1_score(y_test, p_test >= t))

Your calibrated model was trained when fraud was 1%. Costs are unchanged ($t^* = 0.024$), but fraud has risen to 2% in production. What should happen to the decision rule?

If you've moved the threshold and the model still can't hit a useful operating point, the ranking itself is the problem, and that's when changing training is worth it. Part 4 covers class weights and focal loss.
