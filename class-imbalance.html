Dealing with Class Imbalance: Metrics, Thresholds, Weights & Calibration

In fraud detection, rare-disease screening, and ad click prediction, the positive events you care about might make up only $\pi = 1\%$ of your data ($100$ frauds out of $10{,}000$ transactions). If you evaluate a classifier on that dataset using standard accuracy, a dummy model that predicts "never fraud" on every single row scores $99\%$ accuracy while catching zero fraud.

Even worse, standard ROC-AUC and specificity can lull you into thinking a detector is ready for production when its alerts are mostly noise. And when engineers try to fix low recall at the default $0.50$ threshold by aggressively oversampling positives or running SMOTE across their dataset, they often destroy their model's probability calibration—or accidentally leak duplicate minority rows across train and validation folds (data leakage).

Handling imbalanced data cleanly comes down to three disciplined rules: measure performance with PR-AUC and expected dollar cost on natural-distribution splits, move your decision threshold $t^*$ using the cost ratio before touching the training loop, and if you do train with class weights $w_+$ or undersampling, subtract $\ln w_+$ from the output log-odds afterward to recover true calibrated probabilities.

1. The False-Alarm Flood: Why ROC-AUC Flatters Rare-Event Models

To see how a $1\%$ base rate distorts intuition, follow a cohort of $10{,}000$ transactions containing $100$ real frauds and $9{,}900$ legitimate purchases. Suppose your detector catches $90\%$ of all frauds (recall / TPR $= 0.90$) and clears $95\%$ of all legitimate transactions (specificity $= 0.95$, so false positive rate $\text{FPR} = 0.05$). On an ROC chart, $90\%$ TPR at $5\%$ FPR looks fantastic ($\text{ROC-AUC} > 0.95$). What happens when the fraud team opens the alert queue?

Worked Example (Tracing the $2 \times 2$ confusion matrix at a $1\%$ base rate):

1. Among the $100$ real frauds ($90\%$ recall): The model catches $TP = \mathbf{90}$ frauds and misses $FN = \mathbf{10}$.
2. Among the $9{,}900$ legitimate transactions ($5\%$ FPR): A $5\%$ false alarm rate on $9{,}900$ legit purchases generates $FP = 0.05 \times 9{,}900 = \mathbf{495\text{ false alarms}}$ (clearing $TN = 9{,}405$).
3. Precision among the $90 + 495 = 585$ total alerts: $\text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP} = \frac{90}{585} \approx \mathbf{15.4\%}$! Fewer than $1$ in $6$ alerts is real fraud.

Why did ROC-AUC hide those $495$ false alarms? Because ROC plots TPR against $\text{FPR} = \frac{FP}{FP + TN}$, dividing the $495$ false alarms by the massive pool of $9{,}900$ true negatives. PR-AUC instead plots Precision ($\frac{TP}{TP + FP}$) against Recall ($\frac{TP}{TP + FN}$)—never using $TN$ at all—so it exposes false-alarm floods immediately. (And to keep your validation PR-AUC honest, always use stratified splits and never resample or apply SMOTE to your validation/test set or before splitting.)

2. Move the Threshold First ($t^*$), Then Recover Probabilities After Weighting

When a classifier trained on $1\%$ fraud data predicts zero positives at the default $0.50$ threshold, most people immediately start oversampling the training data. That is backwards! When only $1\%$ of transactions are fraudulent, a well-calibrated model should output probabilities around $0.01\text{ to }0.30$.

If a false alarm costs $C_{FP}$ (say, a $\$10$ review fee) and a missed fraud costs $C_{FN}$ (say, a $\$90$ chargeback), you don't need to retrain the model at all—just lower the decision threshold from $0.50$ to the Bayes-optimal cutoff $t^*$:

$$t^* = \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP} + C_{FN}}, \qquad \text{logit}(\hat{p}_{\text{true}}) = \text{logit}(\tilde{p}_{\text{weighted}}) - \ln w_+ \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \text{Odds}_{\text{true}} = \frac{\text{Odds}_{\text{weighted}}}{w_+}$$

With $C_{FP} = \$10$ and $C_{FN} = \$90$, the optimal threshold is $t^* = \frac{10}{10 + 90} = \mathbf{0.10}$: flag any transaction whose calibrated fraud probability exceeds $10\%$.

What if you do upweight the positive class during training by $w_+ = 9$ (or undersample negatives by $9\times$ to save compute on massive datasets)? Upweighting positives by $w_+$ multiplies the model's predicted odds $\frac{\tilde{p}}{1 - \tilde{p}}$ by $w_+$, inflating raw probability outputs toward $50\%$. Before plugging those scores into $t^*$ or showing them to a user, you divide the predicted odds by $w_+$ (which is simply subtracting $\ln w_+$ from the logit) to restore true calibrated probabilities.

3. Comparing Imbalance Interventions

Intervention Where It Acts Distorts Calibration? When to Use / Tradeoff
Cost-Optimal Threshold ($t^*$) Post-training decision rule ($\hat{p} \ge \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP}+C_{FN}}$) No (keeps true $\hat{p}$) Always try first: zero retraining cost, optimal under calibrated probabilities
Class Weights ($w_+$) Multiplies positive loss term by $w_+$ Yes (inflates odds by $w_+$; fix via $-\ln w_+$) When gradient signal from rare positives is too weak; clean & adds zero extra rows
Majority Undersampling Drops $(1 - 1/w_+)$ of training negatives Yes (inflates odds by $w_+$; fix via $-\ln w_+$) Massive datasets ($10\text{M}+$ rows) where dropping redundant negatives speeds up training $10\times$
SMOTE / Oversampling Synthesizes or duplicates minority training rows Yes (warps local density & slows training) Rarely needed over class weights; never apply before train/val split (leakage!)
