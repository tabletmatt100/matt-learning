Softmax and Temperature: Turning Logits into Choices

TL;DR

  • A language model outputs raw, unnormalized scores called logits, not probabilities.
  • Softmax exponentiates each logit and divides by the sum so the scores become positive and add up to 1.
  • Temperature $T$ divides the logits before exponentiating: low $T$ sharpens the peak toward greedy decoding, while high $T$ flattens the distribution toward uniform random choice.
  • Temperature does not change which token ranks first; it only changes how much probability mass the runner-ups get.

1. From raw scores to probabilities

Suppose you prompt a language model with "The capital of France is". Internally, the final linear layer scores every token in the vocabulary. Those raw scores are logits: real numbers that can be negative, zero, or arbitrarily large.

To sample a next token, you need a valid probability distribution: every value must be non-negative, and the whole vocabulary must sum to 1. The softmax function with temperature $T > 0$ does both at once:

$$p_i = \frac{\exp(z_i / T)}{\sum_{j=1}^{V} \exp(z_j / T)}$$

Exponentiating turns any real number into a positive one and amplifies differences between scores. Dividing by the sum forces the total to 1. Dividing $z_i$ by $T$ beforehand scales all gaps between logits before the exponential magnifies them.

Math note: shift invariance and the $T \to 0$ / $T \to \infty$ limits

1. Shift invariance: Adding any constant $c$ to every logit leaves softmax unchanged: $$\frac{\exp((z_i + c)/T)}{\sum_j \exp((z_j + c)/T)} = \frac{e^{c/T}\exp(z_i/T)}{e^{c/T}\sum_j \exp(z_j/T)} = \frac{\exp(z_i/T)}{\sum_j \exp(z_j/T)}$$ In code, libraries always subtract $m = \max_j z_j$ from every logit before calling exp. That keeps the largest exponent at $e^0 = 1$ and prevents floating-point overflow.

2. Low-temperature limit ($T \to 0^+$): If $z_1$ is strictly larger than all other logits, then $(z_1 - z_j)/T \to +\infty$ for $j \ne 1$, so $p_1 \to 1$ and every other $p_j \to 0$. That is greedy decoding.

3. High-temperature limit ($T \to \infty$): Every $z_i / T \to 0$, so $\exp(z_i / T) \to 1$, and $p_i \to 1/V$ (a uniform distribution over the vocabulary).

2. Worked example with four tokens

Assume a toy 4-token vocabulary with logits $z = [4.0,\; 2.5,\; 1.5,\; 0.0]$ for Paris, Lyon, London, and pizza. Let us compute the exact probabilities at $T = 1.0$, $T = 0.5$, and $T = 2.0$.

At $T = 1.0$ (unscaled):

$e^{4.0} \approx 54.60$, $\;e^{2.5} \approx 12.18$, $\;e^{1.5} \approx 4.48$, $\;e^{0.0} = 1.00$.
Sum $= 54.60 + 12.18 + 4.48 + 1.00 = 72.26$.

At $T = 0.5$ (sharpened):

Logits double to $[8.0,\; 5.0,\; 3.0,\; 0.0]$.
$e^{8.0} \approx 2980.96$, $\;e^{5.0} \approx 148.41$, $\;e^{3.0} \approx 20.09$, $\;e^{0.0} = 1.00$.
Sum $= 3150.46$. Notice how Paris jumps from 75.6% to 94.6%.

Token Logit $z_i$ $T = 0.5$ $T = 1.0$ $T = 2.0$
Paris 4.0 94.6% 75.6% 51.5%
Lyon 2.5 4.7% 16.9% 24.3%
London 1.5 0.6% 6.2% 14.8%
pizza 0.0 0.03% 1.4% 7.0%

Notice the catch at $T = 2.0$: pizza rises to 7.0%. Over a 100-token response, a 7% chance of picking an absurd token at each step almost guarantees nonsense unless you also clip the tail with top-p or top-k sampling.
