Probability Calibration: When 80% Actually Means 80%

When a binary classifier outputs a score of $0.80$, we naturally want to read that number as an $80\%$ chance that the label is positive. Surprisingly often, that number is a lie. A deep neural network or a boosted tree ensemble can achieve a stellar $0.95$ ROC-AUC—ranking almost every positive case above every negative case—while pushing its raw scores into distorted extremes where a predicted "$0.95$" only comes true $60\%$ of the time.

Why do models drift out of calibration even when their accuracy looks great? Because different training objectives warp probability scores in opposite directions. Over-parameterized neural networks trained with cross-entropy keep pushing logits further apart long after training accuracy hits $100\%$, making them systematically overconfident (scores huddled near $0$ and $1$). By contrast, random forests average the votes of many noisy trees, which makes it hard for all trees to agree on $0\%$ or $100\%$ and leaves their scores underconfident (pushed toward the middle).

Whenever a downstream decision multiplies your model's output by a dollar cost, chains two models together, or displays a risk percentage to a doctor or customer, ranking is no longer enough. We need to diagnose miscalibration with a reliability diagram and Expected Calibration Error (ECE), and fix the probabilities on a held-out validation set using Platt scaling, temperature scaling, or isotonic regression.

1. Ranking Is Not Probability (and Sharpness vs. Calibration)

Suppose a weather app predicts a $70\%$ chance of rain on $100$ different days, and it rains on $70$ of them. That forecast is calibrated: among all examples assigned score $p$, the empirical fraction of positives is $p$:

$$P(Y = 1 \mid \hat{p} = p) = p \quad \text{for all } p \in [0, 1]$$

You do not always need calibrated probabilities. If your only job is to send the top $50$ highest-scoring leads to a sales team each morning, only the rank order matters (ROC-AUC). Calibration becomes mandatory the moment a downstream rule treats $\hat{p}$ as a real probability—such as flagging a transaction whenever expected fraud loss $\hat{p} \times \$500$ exceeds a $\$25$ review fee. If your model's $\hat{p}$ is inflated $3\times$, you will review $3\times$ too many low-risk transactions and lose money.

Notice one subtle catch: a lazy model that predicts the dataset base rate $\pi = 0.20$ for every single row is $100\%$ calibrated (out of everyone given $0.20$, exactly $20\%$ are positive), yet it is completely useless because it has zero sharpness (discrimination). Good probabilistic models need both: high discrimination to separate positives from negatives, and calibration so the numbers tell the truth.

2. Measuring Miscalibration: Reliability Diagrams, ECE & Brier Score

To check whether a model is calibrated on a test set of $N$ examples, group the predictions into $M$ confidence bins (for example, $[0.0, 0.5]$ and $(0.5, 1.0]$). Inside each bin $B_m$, compute the average predicted confidence $\text{conf}(B_m)$ and the actual fraction of positives $\text{acc}(B_m)$. Plotting $\text{acc}(B_m)$ against $\text{conf}(B_m)$ gives a reliability diagram, where a perfectly calibrated model sits right on the diagonal $y = x$.

An overconfident deep neural network predicts $0.90$ when the true positive rate is only $0.65$ (its curve sags below the diagonal at the top right), while an underconfident random forest predicts $0.65$ when the true rate is $0.90$ (forming an S-curve that sits above the diagonal at the right). We summarize that gap with Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Brier Score:

$$\text{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{N} \,\big|\text{acc}(B_m) - \text{conf}(B_m)\big|, \qquad \text{Brier} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} (\hat{p}_i - y_i)^2$$

Worked Example (Computing 2-bin ECE by hand on $N = 100$ test rows):

• Bin 1 ($|B_1| = 60$ rows): Mean predicted confidence $\text{conf}(B_1) = \mathbf{0.20}$, actual positive fraction $\text{acc}(B_1) = \mathbf{0.15}$ (gap $= |0.15 - 0.20| = 0.05$).
• Bin 2 ($|B_2| = 40$ rows): Mean predicted confidence $\text{conf}(B_2) = \mathbf{0.80}$, actual positive fraction $\text{acc}(B_2) = \mathbf{0.60}$ (gap $= |0.60 - 0.80| = 0.20$).
Weighting each bin's gap by its share of the $100$ rows gives $\text{ECE} = \frac{60}{100}(0.05) + \frac{40}{100}(0.20) = 0.03 + 0.08 = \mathbf{0.11}$ ($11\%$ average calibration error).

In a reliability diagram (x-axis $=$ predicted confidence $\text{conf}$, y-axis $=$ empirical positive fraction $\text{acc}$), a model's bin at $\text{conf} = 0.90$ sits at $\text{acc} = 0.60$ (well below the $y = x$ diagonal). What does that tell you?

3. Three Post-Hoc Fixes (Always Fit on a Held-Out Validation Set!)

Whichever calibration method you pick, one rule is absolute: never fit a calibrator on the same training set used to train the classifier. On the training set, an overfit model's high predictions are artificially right $100\%$ of the time, so fitting a calibrator on training data will make overconfidence even worse. Always fit the calibrator on a separate validation set (or out-of-fold predictions).

When you have a binary classifier with sigmoid-shaped distortion and a small validation set ($200\text{–}2{,}000$ rows), Platt Scaling fits a 2-parameter logistic regression $\hat{p}_{\text{cal}} = \sigma(a z + b)$ on the raw logits $z$. When you have a multiclass deep neural network, Temperature Scaling divides the logit vector by a single learned scalar $T > 1$ before softmax ($\text{softmax}(z / T)$); because dividing all logits by $T > 0$ never changes which class has the largest logit, temperature scaling fixes neural net overconfidence while keeping top-1 accuracy and ROC-AUC $100\%$ identical! And when you have a massive validation set ($10{,}000+$ rows) with non-sigmoid distortion, Isotonic Regression fits a free-form non-decreasing step function—though it will overfit on small validation sets.
