Backpropagation From Scratch: How Gradients Flow Through Graphs & Matrices

TL;DR

  • Why not just wiggle each weight? Evaluating the loss $L$ after nudging each of $d$ weights one by one (finite differences) requires $d + 1$ full forward passes. Backpropagation (reverse-mode autodiff) gets all $d$ exact partial derivatives in one forward pass plus one backward pass.
  • Every node obeys one local rule: A node receives an upstream gradient $\frac{\partial L}{\partial z}$ from its output, multiplies it by its local derivative $\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}$, and passes the product backward (Chain Rule). If a variable feeds two branches, you add the incoming gradients from both branches.
  • Forward values become backward multipliers: In $z = w \cdot x + b$, the backward gradient into $w$ multiplies by the cached forward activation $x$, while the backward gradient into $x$ multiplies by $w$. That is why training requires storing intermediate activations in memory (and why inference does not).
  • Where it breaks: Any local derivative equal to $0$ (like a negative input into ReLU) kills all upstream signal behind it (dead neuron), and multiplying many local slopes $< 1$ or $> 1$ across deep layers causes vanishing or exploding gradients unless residual connections create an additive $+1$ highway.

2. The three local gate rules & full computation graph

Every neural network—whether a 2-layer MLP or a 70-billion-parameter Transformer—decomposes into a computation graph built from three wiring patterns. During the backward pass, each gate only needs to know its own local rule:

Gate TypeForward RuleLocal DerivativeBackward Rule (Given Upstream $\bar{z}$)Plain-English Behavior
Add Gate ($+$) $z = a + b$ $\frac{\partial z}{\partial a} = 1,\; \frac{\partial z}{\partial b} = 1$ $\bar{a} = \bar{z},\quad \bar{b} = \bar{z}$ Gradient distributor: copies $\bar{z}$ unchanged to every input branch.
Multiply Gate ($\times$) $z = w \cdot x$ $\frac{\partial z}{\partial w} = x,\; \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = w$ $\bar{w} = x \cdot \bar{z},\quad \bar{x} = w \cdot \bar{z}$ Partner swapper: multiplies $\bar{z}$ by the other input's cached forward value.
Max / ReLU Gate $a = \max(0, z)$ $1$ if $z > 0$, else $0$ $\bar{z} = \begin{cases}\bar{a} & z > 0 \\ 0 & z \le 0\end{cases}$ Gradient switch: passes $\bar{a}$ straight through if active; blocks it to $0$ if negative.
Fan-Out Split (Branch) $u$ feeds both $z_1$ and $z_2$ Multivariable sum $\bar{u} = \bar{u}_{\text{from } z_1} + \bar{u}_{\text{from } z_2}$ Gradient adder: if one variable is used twice forward, add its backward gradients.

Worked numerical trace through a 4-stage neuron:

Take distinct numbers: input $x = 2$, weight $w = 3$, bias $b = -1$, target $y = 2$, with ReLU activation $a = \max(0, z)$ and squared error $L = \frac{1}{2}(a - y)^2$.

Forward pass (left to right — compute and cache):
1. Product node: $u = w \cdot x = 3 \times 2 = \mathbf{6}$ (cache $x = 2, w = 3$).
2. Pre-activation: $z = u + b = 6 + (-1) = \mathbf{5}$.
3. ReLU activation: $a = \max(0, 5) = \mathbf{5}$ (cache active mask $z > 0 \Rightarrow 1$).
4. Loss: $L = \frac{1}{2}(5 - 2)^2 = \frac{1}{2}(9) = \mathbf{4.5}$.

Backward pass (right to left — multiply local slopes):
1. Base seed: $\bar{L} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial L} = \mathbf{1}$.
2. Loss derivative: $\bar{a} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial a} = a - y = 5 - 2 = \mathbf{+3}$.
3. Through ReLU ($z = 5 > 0$, slope $= 1$): $\bar{z} = \bar{a} \cdot 1 = \mathbf{+3}$.
4. Through Add gate ($z = u + b$, slopes $= 1, 1$): $\bar{u} = \mathbf{+3}$ and $\bar{b} = \mathbf{+3}$.
5. Through Multiply gate ($u = w \cdot x$, swap partners): $\bar{w} = x \cdot \bar{u} = 2 \times 3 = \mathbf{+6}$, and $\bar{x} = w \cdot \bar{u} = 3 \times 3 = \mathbf{+9}$.

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial a}}_{a - y \,=\, 3} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial a}{\partial z}}_{\mathbb{1}[z > 0] \,=\, 1} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial z}{\partial u}}_{1} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial u}{\partial w}}_{x \,=\, 2} = 3 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 = +6$$
Verification check: if we nudge $w$ up by $+0.01$ (from $3$ to $3.01$), $z$ rises by $x \cdot 0.01 = +0.02$ (to $5.02$), and $L = \frac{1}{2}(3.02)^2 = 4.5602$ — an increase of $+0.0602 \approx (+6)(0.01)$.

3. Matrix backpropagation without index headaches

In real code, layers operate on vectors and matrices: $z = Wx + b$, where $W$ has shape $(m \times n)$, $x$ has shape $(n \times 1)$, and $z$ and $\bar{z}$ have shape $(m \times 1)$.

You never need to memorize 4D tensor Jacobians to write the backward pass of a linear layer. Because $\bar{W}$ must have the exact same shape as $W$ $(m \times n)$ and $\bar{x}$ must have the exact same shape as $x$ $(n \times 1)$, there is only one way to multiply $\bar{z}$ $(m \times 1)$ with $x$ $(n \times 1)$ or $W$ $(m \times n)$ so the inner dimensions match:

$$\underbrace{\bar{W}}_{(m \times n)} = \underbrace{\bar{z}}_{(m \times 1)} \underbrace{x^\top}_{(1 \times n)} \quad (\text{outer product}), \qquad \underbrace{\bar{x}}_{(n \times 1)} = \underbrace{W^\top}_{(n \times m)} \underbrace{\bar{z}}_{(m \times 1)}, \qquad \underbrace{\bar{b}}_{(m \times 1)} = \underbrace{\bar{z}}_{(m \times 1)}$$
Scalar vs. matrix correspondence: in 1D, $\bar{w} = \bar{z} \cdot x$ and $\bar{x} = w \cdot \bar{z}$. In matrix form, transposing $x^\top$ and $W^\top$ is the only arrangement where matrix multiplication is legal.

Worked $2 \times 2$ matrix backprop trace (distinct, traceable numbers):

Let input vector $x$ have 2 features, weight matrix $W$ map 2 inputs to 2 outputs, and upstream gradient $\bar{z}$ arrive from the next layer:

$$W = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad x = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \bar{z} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$$

1. Weight gradient $\bar{W} = \bar{z} x^\top$ (each row $i$ scales $x^\top$ by $\bar{z}_i$):

$$\bar{W} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \times 5 & 2 \times 6 \\ -1 \times 5 & -1 \times 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{10} & \mathbf{12} \\ \mathbf{-5} & \mathbf{-6} \end{bmatrix}$$

2. Input gradient $\bar{x} = W^\top \bar{z}$ (each input $j$ sums downstream gradients weighted by column $j$ of $W$):

$$\bar{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1(2) + 3(-1) \\ 2(2) + 4(-1) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 - 3 \\ 4 - 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{-1} \\ \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix}$$

Notice why $\bar{x}_1 = -1$ puts two numbers together: input $x_1 = 5$ fed both output $z_1$ (via $W_{11}=1$) and output $z_2$ (via $W_{21}=3$). Multiplying by $W^\top$ automatically sums both branches!

4. Why deep chains vanish or explode (and how residuals fix it)

In an $L$-layer plain network $h_\ell = f(w_\ell h_{\ell-1})$, the Chain Rule multiplies $L$ local derivatives in a row:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial h_0} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_L} \cdot \prod_{\ell=1}^{L} \frac{\partial h_\ell}{\partial h_{\ell-1}}$$

For a sigmoid gate $\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$, the local derivative is $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))$, which reaches a maximum of only $\mathbf{0.25}$ at $z = 0$ (and shrinks toward $0$ when saturated). Across $10$ layers with unit weights, multiplying $0.25^{10} \approx 9.5 \times 10^{-7}$ erases the gradient before it reaches early layers (vanishing gradient). Conversely, if each layer's slope is $1.5$, then $1.5^{10} \approx 57.7$ grows exponentially (exploding gradient).

A residual connection changes each block from $h_\ell = F(h_{\ell-1})$ to $h_\ell = h_{\ell-1} + F(h_{\ell-1})$. By the Add Gate rule, the local derivative becomes $\mathbf{1 + F'(h_{\ell-1})}$, creating a direct additive highway that carries upstream gradients intact even when $F'$ is tiny.

Why the Cross-Entropy + Softmax backward pass simplifies to $\hat{p} - y$

At the output layer of a classifier, logits $z$ pass through softmax $\hat{p}_i = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}$ into cross-entropy loss $L = -\sum_k y_k \ln \hat{p}_k$ (where $y$ is a one-hot target with $\sum_k y_k = 1$).

Taking the derivative of $L = -\sum_k y_k \left(z_k - \ln \sum_j e^{z_j}\right)$ with respect to logit $z_i$ directly:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i} = -y_i + \left(\sum_k y_k\right) \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}} = -y_i + 1 \cdot \hat{p}_i = \hat{p}_i - y_i$$

Instead of multiplying a tiny softmax slope by a huge $1/\hat{p}$ loss derivative (which risks numerical instability), the two cancel analytically into $\bar{z} = \hat{p} - y$. If the true class is $1$ and the model predicts $\hat{p}_1 = 0.80$, the gradient on logit $z_1$ is simply $0.80 - 1.00 = -0.20$.

Stage 4B: Closed-Book Retrieval (Blank-Sheet Test)

Why Does Training Use 3–4x More Memory Than Inference?

Without scrolling up, explain in one or two sentences why a neural network must keep intermediate forward activations $x_\ell$ in GPU/RAM during the forward pass when training, whereas inference can discard each layer's activations immediately.

Canonical answer: At every linear or multiply gate $z_\ell = W_\ell x_\ell + b_\ell$, the local derivative with respect to the weights is the forward activation itself ($\bar{W}_\ell = \bar{z}_\ell x_\ell^\top$), and the derivative through nonlinearities like ReLU or Sigmoid depends on the forward pre-activation $z_\ell$. During training, every layer's forward activations $x_\ell$ must stay cached in memory until the backward pass reaches layer $\ell$ (unless activation checkpointing recomputes them), whereas inference never runs a backward pass and overwrites activation buffers immediately.
