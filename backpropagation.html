Backpropagation From Scratch: How Gradients Flow Through Graphs & Matrices

Suppose a neural network with one million weights makes a prediction on an image and gets a high loss $L$. How do you figure out which direction to nudge each of those one million weights to make the loss smaller?

The brute-force approach—nudging weight $w_1$ by $+0.0001$, running the entire network forward to see if the loss went up or down, and repeating that for $w_2, w_3, \dots, w_{1{,}000{,}000}$ (finite differences)—would require one million separate forward passes just to take a single gradient step. Training a modern language model that way would take longer than the age of the universe.

Backpropagation (reverse-mode automatic differentiation) computes the exact partial derivative for all one million weights in one forward pass plus one backward sweep. Instead of treating the network as a giant algebraic formula, it breaks every layer down into tiny elementary gates ($+$, $\times$, $\max$). On the forward pass, each gate caches its input values; on the backward pass, each gate receives an upstream gradient from the right, multiplies it by its own local derivative (Chain Rule), and hands the result to the left.

1. The Four Local Gate Rules (and Why Training Uses 3x More Memory Than Inference)

Every neural network—whether a 2-layer MLP or a 70-billion-parameter Transformer—decomposes into a computation graph built from four simple local wiring patterns. During the backward pass, each gate only cares about its own immediate inputs and the upstream gradient $\bar{z} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z}$ arriving from its output:

First, an Add Gate ($z = a + b$) has local slopes $\frac{\partial z}{\partial a} = 1$ and $\frac{\partial z}{\partial b} = 1$, so it acts as a gradient distributor, copying $\bar{z}$ unchanged to both branches ($\bar{a} = \bar{z}$ and $\bar{b} = \bar{z}$). Second, a Multiply Gate ($z = w \cdot x$) has local slopes $\frac{\partial z}{\partial w} = x$ and $\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = w$, so it acts as a partner swapper: the gradient into the weight multiplies by the cached input ($\bar{w} = x \cdot \bar{z}$), and the gradient into the input multiplies by the weight ($\bar{x} = w \cdot \bar{z}$).

That partner-swap rule at every multiply gate explains why training a neural network consumes several times more GPU memory than running inference. During inference, you can overwrite layer 1's activations $x_1$ as soon as layer 2 finishes. During training, you must keep every layer's forward activations $x_\ell$ cached in memory until the backward sweep reaches layer $\ell$, because $\bar{w}_\ell = x_\ell \cdot \bar{z}_\ell$ cannot be computed without $x_\ell$!

Third, a ReLU Gate ($a = \max(0, z)$) acts as a gradient switch: if the forward input was positive ($z > 0$), its slope is $1$ and $\bar{z} = \bar{a}$ passes straight through; if $z \le 0$, its slope is $0$ and the gate blocks the gradient completely ($\bar{z} = 0$). Finally, if a variable fans out to feed two different branches on the forward pass, the multivariable Chain Rule says you simply add the backward gradients returning from both branches.

Worked Example (Tracing forward cache & backward gradients through a 4-stage neuron):

Take distinct numbers: input $x = 2$, weight $w = 3$, bias $b = -1$, target $y = 2$, with ReLU activation $a = \max(0, wx + b)$ and squared error loss $L = \frac{1}{2}(a - y)^2$:
1. Forward pass (left to right — compute and cache): Product $u = w \cdot x = 3(2) = \mathbf{6}$ (cache $x=2, w=3$). Pre-activation $z = u + b = 6 + (-1) = \mathbf{5}$ (cache active ReLU mask $z > 0$). Activation $a = \max(0, 5) = \mathbf{5}$. Loss $L = \frac{1}{2}(5 - 2)^2 = \mathbf{4.5}$.
2. Backward pass (right to left — multiply local slopes): Loss derivative $\bar{a} = a - y = 5 - 2 = \mathbf{+3}$. Through open ReLU switch ($z = 5 > 0$): $\bar{z} = \mathbf{+3}$. Through Add gate ($z = u + b$): $\bar{u} = \mathbf{+3}$ and $\bar{b} = \mathbf{+3}$. Through Multiply gate ($u = w \cdot x$, swap partners): $\bar{w} = x \cdot \bar{u} = 2 \times 3 = \mathbf{+6}$, and $\bar{x} = w \cdot \bar{u} = 3 \times 3 = \mathbf{+9}$.

In a linear + ReLU layer $z = w \cdot x + b$, $a = \max(0, z)$, suppose on the forward pass $x = 0$, $w = 4$, $b = 3$, and the upstream gradient arriving at $a$ is $\bar{a} = +5$. What are the gradients $\bar{w} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial w}$ and $\bar{x} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial x}$?

Suppose weight $w = 2$ feeds two parallel paths that add together: $u = 3w$, $v = w^2$, and $L = u + v$. At $w = 2$, what is the total gradient $\bar{w} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial w}$?

2. Matrix Backpropagation Without Index Headaches

In real code, layers operate on vectors and matrices: $z = Wx + b$, where $W$ has shape $(m \times n)$, $x$ has shape $(n \times 1)$, and $z$ and its upstream gradient $\bar{z}$ have shape $(m \times 1)$.

You never need to memorize 4D tensor Jacobians to write the backward pass of a matrix multiply. Because the weight gradient $\bar{W}$ must have the exact same shape as $W$ $(m \times n)$ and the input gradient $\bar{x}$ must have the exact same shape as $x$ $(n \times 1)$, there is only one legal way to multiply $\bar{z}$ $(m \times 1)$ with $x$ $(n \times 1)$ or $W$ $(m \times n)$ so their inner dimensions align:

$$\underbrace{\bar{W}}_{(m \times n)} = \underbrace{\bar{z}}_{(m \times 1)} \underbrace{x^\top}_{(1 \times n)} \quad (\text{outer product}), \qquad \underbrace{\bar{x}}_{(n \times 1)} = \underbrace{W^\top}_{(n \times m)} \underbrace{\bar{z}}_{(m \times 1)}, \qquad \underbrace{\bar{b}}_{(m \times 1)} = \underbrace{\bar{z}}_{(m \times 1)}$$

Worked Example ($2 \times 2$ matrix backprop trace with distinct numbers):

Let $W = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$, input $x = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$, and upstream gradient $\bar{z} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$:
1. Weight gradient $\bar{W} = \bar{z} x^\top$: $\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2(5) & 2(6) \\ -1(5) & -1(6) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{10} & \mathbf{12} \\ \mathbf{-5} & \mathbf{-6} \end{bmatrix}$.
2. Input gradient $\bar{x} = W^\top \bar{z}$: $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1(2) + 3(-1) \\ 2(2) + 4(-1) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{-1} \\ \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix}$. Notice why $W^\top$ appears naturally: input $x_1 = 5$ fed both $z_1$ (via $W_{11}=1$) and $z_2$ (via $W_{21}=3$), so multiplying by row 1 of $W^\top$ automatically sums both fan-out branches!

A linear layer computes $z = Wx + b$ where $W$ has shape $(4 \times 3)$, $x$ has shape $(3 \times 1)$, and $z$ has shape $(4 \times 1)$. Given upstream gradient $\bar{z}$ of shape $(4 \times 1)$, which expression computes $\bar{x} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial x}$?
