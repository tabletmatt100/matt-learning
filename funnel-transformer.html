Funnel-Transformer: Why Keep Every Word Separate Across Every Layer?

Think about how you read a sentence when your only job is to decide whether a movie review is positive or negative. At the start, you look at individual words. Within a split second, you group those words into phrases ("not very good"), and then into a single takeaway. You don't carry every individual word vector separately all the way to the end of your thought process.

Standard Transformer encoders like BERT do the exact opposite. If you feed BERT $T = 512$ tokens, Layer 1 processes 512 tokens, Layer 6 processes 512 tokens, and Layer 12 processes 512 tokens—even though for sequence classification you throw 511 of those output vectors in the trash and only read the final [CLS] token. By the middle layers, neighboring tokens have already blended their information together via self-attention, so keeping 512 separate vectors is mostly wasted math.

In 2020, Zihang Dai and colleagues asked an obvious question: why not shrink the sequence length as you go deeper, the same way a convolutional network shrinks an image grid from $256 \times 256$ down to $64 \times 64$? Their model, Funnel-Transformer, cuts the number of tokens in half every few layers, shaping the encoder like a funnel ($T \to T/2 \to T/4$).

1. The Naive Pooling Trap (and the "Pool-Query-Only" Fix)

The simplest way to cut a sequence of hidden states $h$ in half is stride-2 average pooling: average tokens 1 and 2 into one vector, average tokens 3 and 4 into the next vector, and call that half-length sequence $h' = \text{Pool}(h)$.

If you simply replace $h$ with $h'$ and run normal self-attention on $h'$, accuracy drops noticeably. Why? Because plain average pooling is blind. If token 1 is a high-signal word like "not" (value $v_1 = 2$) and token 2 is a filler word like "very" (value $v_2 = 10$), a 50/50 average smashes them into $\frac{2+10}{2} = 6$ before attention ever gets to decide which word mattered more.

Funnel-Transformer fixes this at the boundary layer where the sequence shrinks. In attention, the output sequence always has the exact same length as the queries ($Q$), no matter how many keys ($K$) and values ($V$) you look up. So at the transition layer, Funnel-Transformer uses the pooled half-length sequence $h'$ as the queries $Q$ (and as the residual stream base), while keeping the full, unpooled sequence $h$ as the keys $K$ and values $V$ (Pool-Query-Only):

$$h \leftarrow \text{LayerNorm}\Bigl(h' + \text{Attn}\bigl(Q = h',\; K = h,\; V = h\bigr)\Bigr)$$

Because each compressed query in $h'$ attends over the individual unpooled tokens in $h$, the attention head learns how to weight each word during compression instead of settling for a blind 50/50 average. And once that single transition layer finishes, the sequence is officially half as long ($T/2$), so every interior layer after it runs four times faster on attention and twice as fast on the feed-forward network (FFN).

(One practical detail: you never want the global [CLS] token at position 0 to get averaged with the first word of the sentence. So Funnel-Transformer slices [CLS] off before pooling the content tokens and sticks [CLS] back on the front afterward.)

Worked Example (4 tokens $\to$ 2 tokens with distinct mental-math numbers):

Suppose 4 unpooled tokens have values $V = [2,\; 6,\; 10,\; 14]$.
1. Stride-2 pooling ($h \to h'$): Average pairs $(2, 6)$ and $(10, 14)$ into 2 pooled queries: $h'_1 = \frac{2+6}{2} = \mathbf{4}$ and $h'_2 = \frac{10+14}{2} = \mathbf{12}$.
2. Selective compression on pair 2: Suppose query $q_2$ (built from $h'_2 = 12$) attends over unpooled tokens 3 and 4 ($v_3 = 10, v_4 = 14$) with sharp softmax weights $\alpha = [0,\; 0,\; \frac{1}{4},\; \frac{3}{4}]$ instead of $50/50$.
3. Attention + pooled residual: The attention weighted sum is $\frac{1}{4}(10) + \frac{3}{4}(14) = 2.5 + 10.5 = \mathbf{13}$. Adding the pooled residual $h'_2 = 12$ gives the compressed token $h^{\text{out}}_2 = 12 + 13 = \mathbf{25}$.

In the example above, tokens 1 and 2 have unpooled values $v_1 = 2$ ("not") and $v_2 = 10$ ("very"). If query $q_1$ assigns attention weights $\alpha = [\frac{3}{4},\; \frac{1}{4},\; 0,\; 0]$ to those two tokens, what is the attention output $\sum_j \alpha_j v_j$ under Pool-Query-Only compared to a blind 50/50 average?

2. Wait—How Do You Pre-Train It If Most Tokens Are Gone?

Shrinking the sequence to $T/4$ works great when you only care about the final [CLS] vector. Here is the catch: BERT-style encoders are pre-trained with Masked Language Modeling (MLM), where you mask out 15% of the input words and ask the final layer to guess the missing word at every single token position. How can you predict token 7 if the final layer only has a quarter of the tokens left?

You need a decoder that stretches the compressed states back out to full length $T$ during pre-training. Instead of building an expensive U-Net that upsamples step-by-step after every block, the authors did something almost comically simple (Bridge Upsampling):

Suppose your encoder has $M = 3$ blocks, shrinking the sequence from $T \to T/2 \to T/4$. Each vector in the final compressed output $h^{(3)}$ covers a window of $2^{3-1} = 4$ original words. To get back to length $T$, you just duplicate each compressed vector 4 times in a row ($\text{up-sample}(h^{(M)})$).

Of course, four identical copies in a row can't predict four different masked words on their own. So Funnel-Transformer adds a long residual skip connection from $h^{(1)}$—the last full-length layer at the end of Block 1—straight onto those duplicated vectors, and runs 2 standard Transformer layers on top to blend them into $g$:

$$g = h^{(1)} + \text{up-sample}\bigl(h^{(M)}\bigr)$$

The upsampled vector $\text{up-sample}(h^{(M)})$ supplies the deep, whole-sentence context, while the skip connection $h^{(1)}$ restores the distinct local word identity for each slot. Once pre-training finishes, if you are fine-tuning on a sentence classification task, you throw the 2-layer decoder away completely and only load the funnel encoder.

A 4-token phrase passes through a 3-block Funnel encoder ($T = 4 \to 2 \to 1$). At the end of Block 1 (full length 4), the token vectors are $h^{(1)} = [1,\; 3,\; 5,\; 7]$. At the end of Block 3 (length 1), the single compressed vector is $h^{(3)} = [10]$. What is the fused 4-token sequence $g = h^{(1)} + \text{up-sample}(h^{(3)})$?

3. What Do You Do With the Saved Compute?

Suppose a standard 12-layer BERT model costs 12 units of compute ($1.0$ unit per full-length layer). If you build a 12-layer Funnel-Transformer with three 4-layer blocks at lengths $T$, $T/2$, and $T/4$ (called B4-4-4), each layer at $T/2$ costs at most $0.5$ units and each layer at $T/4$ costs at most $0.25$ units:

$$4 \times (1.0) \;+\; 4 \times (0.5) \;+\; 4 \times (0.25) \;=\; 4 + 2 + 1 \;=\; 7.0\text{ units}$$

You just cut the encoder's compute bill by 42%. In practice, the paper shows two ways to cash that check (FLOP reinvestment):

If you keep the same 12-layer depth (B4-4-4), you get a much faster model that trades away a tiny fraction of a point on token-heavy benchmarks. If instead you reinvest the saved FLOPs by stacking 6 layers in each block (an 18-layer B6-6-6 model), your model is both deeper (18 layers vs. 12) and still faster than a 12-layer BERT—beating it across GLUE, text classification, and reading comprehension.

There is only one place where Funnel-Transformer doesn't fit naturally: causal, left-to-right GPT-style generation. When you pool tokens 1 and 2 together and later upsample them back to position 1, token 2's future information leaks backward into position 1, violating the causal mask. For bidirectional encoders that read the whole prompt at once, however, throwing away token redundancy as you go deeper is practically a free lunch.
