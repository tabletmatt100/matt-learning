The Vanishing Gradient Problem: Why Early Layers and Old Tokens Stop Learning

TL;DR

  • The core failure: Backpropagation multiplies local derivatives step by step from the loss $L$ back to early layers or early time steps. When each step's multiplier is smaller than $1$, a chain of $K$ steps shrinks the gradient exponentially like $c^K \to 0$, freezing early weights while late weights keep moving.
  • In regular DNNs (depth $\ell = 1 \dots L$): Each layer $\ell$ has its own distinct weight $w_\ell$, and the gradient into layer $1$ multiplies $\prod_{\ell=2}^L (w_\ell \cdot f'(z_\ell))$. Because sigmoid's slope $\sigma'(z)$ never exceeds $\frac{1}{4}$, stacking even $4$ sigmoid layers divides the gradient by at least $4^4 = 256$.
  • In RNNs (time $t = 1 \dots T$): Unrolling across $T$ tokens creates a virtual $T$-layer network that reuses the exact same recurrent weight $w_h$ at every step (BPTT). The total gradient is a sum over time steps, but the credit from step $T$ back to token $1$ scales as $(w_h)^T$—either vanishing to $0$ ($|w_h| < 1$) or exploding to $\infty$ ($|w_h| > 1$).
  • The two architectural fixes: Replace saturating activations with ReLU (slope $= 1$ for $z > 0$) plus variance-preserving initialization, and wire an additive $+1$ highway across layers (ResNet $h_\ell = h_{\ell-1} + F(h_{\ell-1})$) or across time (LSTM cell state $c_t = f_t \cdot c_{t-1} + i_t \cdot \tilde{c}_t$).

2. Regular DNNs: how gradients decay across depth

In a feedforward DNN, layer $\ell$ computes pre-activation $z_\ell = w_\ell h_{\ell-1}$ and activation $h_\ell = f(z_\ell)$. By the Chain Rule, passing a gradient backward by one layer multiplies by two factors: the layer's weight $w_\ell$ and its activation slope $f'(z_\ell)$.

Worked 3-layer DNN trace with distinct numbers (easy to follow each factor):

Take a 3-layer network with input $h_0 = 2$, distinct layer weights $w_1 = 3,\; w_2 = 2,\; w_3 = 1$, distinct local activation slopes $f'(z_1) = \frac{1}{2},\; f'(z_2) = \frac{1}{4},\; f'(z_3) = \frac{1}{2}$, and distinct cached forward activations $h_0 = 2,\; h_1 = 3,\; h_2 = 4$. Suppose the loss derivative at the top activation is $\bar{h}_3 = \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_3} = 16$.

1. Top layer ($\ell = 3$) updates fast:
• Through activation $f(z_3)$: $\bar{z}_3 = \bar{h}_3 \cdot f'(z_3) = 16 \times \frac{1}{2} = \mathbf{8}$.
• Weight gradient $\bar{w}_3 = \bar{z}_3 \cdot h_2 = 8 \times 4 = \mathbf{32}$.
• Pass gradient down to layer 2: $\bar{h}_2 = \bar{z}_3 \cdot w_3 = 8 \times 1 = \mathbf{8}$.

2. Middle layer ($\ell = 2$) loses $4\times$ at the activation gate:
• Through activation $f(z_2)$: $\bar{z}_2 = \bar{h}_2 \cdot f'(z_2) = 8 \times \frac{1}{4} = \mathbf{2}$.
• Weight gradient $\bar{w}_2 = \bar{z}_2 \cdot h_1 = 2 \times 3 = \mathbf{6}$.
• Pass gradient down to layer 1: $\bar{h}_1 = \bar{z}_2 \cdot w_2 = 2 \times 2 = \mathbf{4}$.

3. First layer ($\ell = 1$) receives a fraction of the top signal:
• Through activation $f(z_1)$: $\bar{z}_1 = \bar{h}_1 \cdot f'(z_1) = 4 \times \frac{1}{2} = \mathbf{2}$.
• Weight gradient $\bar{w}_1 = \bar{z}_1 \cdot h_0 = 2 \times 2 = \mathbf{4}$ (versus $\bar{w}_3 = 32$ at the top!).

$$\bar{w}_1 = \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} = \underbrace{\bar{h}_3}_{16} \cdot \underbrace{f'(z_3)}_{1/2} \cdot \underbrace{w_3}_{1} \cdot \underbrace{f'(z_2)}_{1/4} \cdot \underbrace{w_2}_{2} \cdot \underbrace{f'(z_1)}_{1/2} \cdot \underbrace{h_0}_{2} = 16 \cdot \frac{1}{8} \cdot 2 = 4$$
General $L$-layer DNN formula: $\bar{w}_1 = \bar{h}_L f'(z_L) \left[\prod_{\ell=2}^{L} w_\ell f'(z_{\ell-1})\right] h_0$. If each layer factor $|w_\ell f'(z_{\ell-1})| \le \frac{1}{4}$, a 10-layer network shrinks $\bar{w}_1$ by $(1/4)^9 = 1/262{,}144$.

Why does sigmoid guarantee decay unless weights are huge? Because $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))$ is a product of two probabilities that add to $1$: its maximum at $z = 0$ is $\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} = \mathbf{0.25}$, and in the saturated tails ($|z| \ge 4$) it drops below $0.018$. Even tanh ($\tanh'(z) = 1 - \tanh^2(z) \le 1$) shrinks gradients at every layer where $z \ne 0$.

3. RNNs: vanishing gradients across time (and why old tokens are forgotten)

A Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) reads a sequence of tokens $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_T$ by updating a hidden state $h_t = f(w_h h_{t-1} + w_x x_t)$ at each step $t$. Notice two big differences from a regular DNN:

  • Weight sharing across time: A 50-layer DNN has 50 distinct weights $w_1, \dots, w_{50}$, whereas an RNN unrolled over 50 tokens reuses the exact same recurrent weight $w_h$ at every single time step.
  • Sum-of-paths gradient (BPTT): Because $w_h$ is used at every step $t = 1 \dots T$, its total gradient $\bar{w}_h$ is the sum of contributions from all time steps. However, the contribution that teaches the network to connect token $t = 1$ to a loss at token $t = T$ must travel through $T - 1$ copies of $w_h \cdot f'(z_k)$.

Worked 3-step RNN trace with distinct inputs $x = [4, 2, 1]$ (mental math, zero calculator):

To isolate the recurrent highway cleanly, take a linear RNN step $h_t = w_h h_{t-1} + w_x x_t$ ($f' = 1$) starting from $h_0 = 0$, with shared recurrent weight $w_h = \frac{1}{2}$, input weight $w_x = 1$, and distinct token inputs $x_1 = 4,\; x_2 = 2,\; x_3 = 1$. A prediction loss $L$ is evaluated at the final step $T = 3$ with upstream gradient $\bar{h}_3 = \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_3} = 4$.

Forward pass across time ($t = 1 \to 2 \to 3$):
• Step $t=1$: $h_1 = \frac{1}{2}(0) + 1(4) = \mathbf{4}$.
• Step $t=2$: $h_2 = \frac{1}{2}(4) + 1(2) = 2 + 2 = \mathbf{4}$.
• Step $t=3$: $h_3 = \frac{1}{2}(4) + 1(1) = 2 + 1 = \mathbf{3}$.

Backward pass through time (BPTT, $t = 3 \to 2 \to 1$):
Since $\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-1}} = w_h = \frac{1}{2}$, each backward step in time halves the hidden-state gradient:
• At $t=3$: $\bar{h}_3 = \mathbf{4}$. Influence of token $x_3$: $\bar{x}_3 = \bar{h}_3 \cdot w_x = \mathbf{4}$.
• At $t=2$: $\bar{h}_2 = \bar{h}_3 \cdot w_h = 4 \times \frac{1}{2} = \mathbf{2}$. Influence of token $x_2$: $\bar{x}_2 = \mathbf{2}$.
• At $t=1$: $\bar{h}_1 = \bar{h}_2 \cdot w_h = 2 \times \frac{1}{2} = \mathbf{1}$. Influence of token $x_1$: $\bar{x}_1 = \mathbf{1}$.

What happens to the shared weight gradient $\bar{w}_h$?
Because $w_h$ was used at $t=2$ (multiplying $h_1 = 4$) and at $t=3$ (multiplying $h_2 = 4$), we add both temporal branches:
$$\bar{w}_h = \underbrace{\bar{h}_3 \cdot h_2}_{\text{step } t=3 \text{ (recent)}} + \underbrace{\bar{h}_2 \cdot h_1}_{\text{step } t=2 \text{ (older)}} = (4 \times 4) + (2 \times 4) = \mathbf{16} + \mathbf{8} = 24$$ Notice the trap: $\bar{w}_h = 24$ is not zero! Instead, it is dominated by recent tokens ($16$ from $t=3$ vs. $8$ from $t=2$, and over $20$ steps a token at $t=1$ contributes $(1/2)^{19} \approx 0.000002$). The RNN updates $w_h$ solely to fix short-term correlations and stays completely blind to long-range dependencies.

$$\frac{\partial L_T}{\partial h_1} = \frac{\partial L_T}{\partial h_T} \prod_{k=2}^{T} \frac{\partial h_k}{\partial h_{k-1}} = \bar{h}_T \cdot w_h^{\,T-1} \prod_{k=2}^{T} f'(z_k)$$
In matrix form with recurrent weight matrix $W_h$, the multiplier $(w_h)^{T-1}$ is governed by the largest eigenvalue / singular value $\rho(W_h)$: if $\rho(W_h) < 1$, long-term signals vanish; if $\rho(W_h) > 1$, they explode.

How do LSTMs (and GRUs) keep token 1 alive across 100 steps? They introduce a separate memory lane called the cell state $c_t = f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde{c}_t$, where the forget gate $f_t \in [0, 1]$ is an additive pass-through multiplier (Constant Error Carousel). When the network sets $f_t \approx 1$ for an important early token, $\frac{\partial c_t}{\partial c_{t-1}} \approx 1$, carrying the gradient back across dozens of steps without decaying. (Modern Transformers go one step further: self-attention connects token $T$ to token $1$ in a single $O(1)$ hop instead of $T-1$ serial multiplications.)

Why the math of Sigmoid's maximum derivative is always $\frac{1}{4}$ (and how Xavier/He init scales weights)

For sigmoid $\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}$, the derivative by the quotient/chain rule is:

$$\sigma'(z) = \frac{e^{-z}}{(1 + e^{-z})^2} = \sigma(z)\bigl(1 - \sigma(z)\bigr)$$

Set $p = \sigma(z) \in (0, 1)$. The parabola $p(1 - p) = \frac{1}{4} - (p - \frac{1}{2})^2$ peaks at $p = \frac{1}{2}$ ($z = 0$), where $\sigma'(0) = \frac{1}{2}(1 - \frac{1}{2}) = \frac{1}{4}$.

Even with ReLU (where $f'(z) = 1$ for $z > 0$), gradients still vanish or explode if weight matrices $W_\ell$ change the variance of vectors from layer to layer. If layer $\ell$ has $n_{\text{in}}$ inputs and roughly half are zeroed by ReLU, keeping forward activation variance and backward gradient variance equal to $1$ requires initializing weights with variance $\text{Var}(W_{ij}) = \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}}}$ (He initialization; for linear/tanh layers without halving, Xavier initialization uses $\frac{1}{n_{\text{in}}}$).

Backward-Faded Worked Example (Plain Block vs. Residual Block)

Compute $\bar{h}_0$ Across Two Blocks When Each Branch Has Local Slope $F' = \frac{1}{2}$

Suppose top gradient $\bar{h}_2 = 16$ flows backward through two blocks ($h_0 \to h_1 \to h_2$) where each learned branch $F_\ell(h_{\ell-1})$ has local derivative $F' = \frac{1}{2}$:
Step 1 (Plain network $h_\ell = F_\ell(h_{\ell-1})$): Each block multiplies by $\frac{1}{2}$, so $\bar{h}_1 = 16 \times \frac{1}{2} = 8$, and $\bar{h}_0 = 8 \times \frac{1}{2} = \mathbf{4}$ (lost $75\%$ of the signal in just 2 blocks).
Step 2 (ResNet block 2: $h_2 = h_1 + F_2(h_1)$): Because of the $+ h_1$ skip wire, the local derivative is $1 + F' = 1 + \frac{1}{2} = \frac{3}{2}$. Thus $\bar{h}_1 = \bar{h}_2 \times \frac{3}{2} = 16 \times \frac{3}{2} = \mathbf{24}$.

Closed-Book Retrieval (Blank-Sheet Test)

DNN Depth Vanishing vs. RNN Temporal Vanishing

Without scrolling up, answer two questions in your own words: (1) In a regular 10-layer DNN, why is it easy to see in the gradient vector that layer 1 has a vanishing gradient, whereas in a 10-step RNN the recurrent weight gradient $\bar{w}_h$ can still be large even when long-term gradients have vanished? (2) Why can you fix exploding gradients with gradient clipping, but cannot fix vanishing gradients by simply multiplying the gradient by $10^6$?

Canonical answer:
1. Distinct weights vs. shared weights: In a 10-layer DNN, layer 1 has its own separate parameter $w_1$, so $\bar{w}_1 \approx 0$ directly shows that early layers are frozen while $\bar{w}_{10}$ is large. In an RNN, the same weight $w_h$ is shared across all 10 time steps, so $\bar{w}_h = \sum_{t=1}^{10} \bar{w}_{h}^{(t)}$ sums recent and old paths together; the short-term gradient from $t=10, 9$ keeps total $\bar{w}_h$ large and hides the fact that the long-term signal from $t=1$ has decayed to zero.
2. Why rescaling fails on vanishing gradients: In an RNN, multiplying $\bar{w}_h$ by $10^6$ blows up the dominant short-term terms from $t=10$ instead of recovering the vanished $t=1$ term; in a deep DNN, once products underflow float32 precision or mix with numerical noise, rescaling amplifies noise and ill-conditioning rather than restoring the true causal direction (you need an architectural $+1$ skip path like ResNet or LSTM).
