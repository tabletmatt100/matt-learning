Class imbalance · Part 5 Resampling

Resampling, SMOTE and balanced ensembles

TL;DR

  • Resampling changes the training data instead of the loss. Most people reach for it first. I'd put it third, after a tuned threshold and class weights.
  • Random undersampling to 1:1 on our data throws away 9,800 of 9,900 legit rows. Partial undersampling is mainly a speed trick.
  • Random oversampling to 1:1 copies every fraud row 99 times. For logistic regression that gives exactly the same objective as a class weight of 99, on a dataset twice the size.
  • SMOTE makes new fraud points on the line between two real ones. It never looks at the majority class, so where the classes overlap it paints fraud labels onto legit territory. It also struggles with one-hot features and high dimensions.
  • Benchmarks with tuned gradient boosting and a tuned threshold find SMOTE adds little. It has to beat that baseline on your folds before you keep it.
  • Every resampler shifts the training prior, so raw probabilities need correcting (Part 6), and resampling must happen inside the training folds only (Part 2).
  • Balanced ensembles undersample many times. Ten balanced subsets see only about 956 distinct legit rows out of 9,900. You need 227 subsets to see 90% of them.

Same setup as the rest of the series: a card-fraud model trained on 10,000 transactions, 100 of them fraud (1%, a 99:1 ratio). When I say "negatives" I mean the 9,900 legit transactions. If you need the metrics background, Part 1 covers why accuracy is useless here and what to watch instead.

1. Random under- and oversampling

Resampling changes the training data instead of the loss. It's the approach most people reach for first, and in my experience it should be about third. The two simplest versions are opposites: delete majority rows, or copy minority rows. Both are one line in imbalanced-learn.

Random undersampling

Random undersampling throws away majority examples until the classes are closer. Going all the way to 100:100 on our data discards 9,800 of 9,900 legit transactions, 99% of your negatives. That's a lot of information about what normal looks like. Partial undersampling (say to 10:1) is more reasonable, and it does have one real advantage: training gets much faster. On a billion-row click log, downsampling negatives is standard practice for that reason alone, followed by a probability correction (Part 6).

Undersample to 10:1. Keep all 100 fraud rows and $100 \times 10 = 1{,}000$ legit rows.

Discarded: $9{,}900 - 1{,}000 = 8{,}900$ legit rows, which is $8{,}900 / 9{,}900 = 89.9\%$ of the negatives.

Training set: $1{,}000 + 100 = 1{,}100$ rows instead of 10,000, about 9 times fewer. The model now sees fraud at $100 / 1{,}100 = 9.09\%$ instead of 1%.

Random oversampling

Random oversampling duplicates minority examples. To reach 50:50 here, each fraud row appears 99 times. Nothing is lost, but nothing new is added either, and flexible models (deep trees, k-NN) will memorise those 100 points. For a linear model, oversampling by a factor $r$ is the same as giving the class a weight of $r$, so you may as well use weights and skip the bigger dataset.

Logistic regression minimises a sum of per-row losses $\ell_i$. With every fraud row copied 99 times:

$$L_{\text{dup}} = \sum_{\text{legit}} \ell_i + \sum_{\text{fraud}} (\underbrace{\ell_i + \dots + \ell_i}_{99\text{ copies}}) = \sum_{\text{legit}} \ell_i + 99 \sum_{\text{fraud}} \ell_i$$

That is term for term the weighted loss with $w_{\text{fraud}} = 99$, $w_{\text{legit}} = 1$. Total mass: $9{,}900 \times 1 + 100 \times 99 = 9{,}900 + 9{,}900$, so the model sees a 50:50 world either way.

Rows stored: 19,800 for the duplicated version, 10,000 for the weighted one.

Where "the same" stops being exactly true

The objectives match for full-batch solvers like sklearn's lbfgs, whose objective is $C\sum_i w_i \ell_i$ plus a penalty. They drift apart with minibatch SGD (a batch of duplicates gives a different gradient noise pattern than one weighted row), with bagging (a bootstrap sample can draw several copies of the same fraud row), and with trees that count rows, for example min_samples_leaf=20 can be satisfied by 20 copies of one transaction. That last one is how oversampled trees memorise.

What sampling_strategy means

In imbalanced-learn, a float sampling_strategy is the minority/majority ratio you want after resampling, not a fraction of rows to keep. RandomUnderSampler(sampling_strategy=0.1) keeps majority rows until $100 / n_{\text{maj}} = 0.1$, so $n_{\text{maj}} = 1{,}000$. RandomOverSampler(sampling_strategy=0.2) adds fraud rows until $n_{\text{min}} / 9{,}900 = 0.2$, so $n_{\text{min}} = 1{,}980$. 2. SMOTE

SMOTE (Chawla et al., 2002) creates new minority points instead of copying. For each minority point, find its $k$ nearest minority neighbours (default $k=5$), pick one, and place a new point at a random spot on the line segment between them:

$$x_{\text{new}} = x_i + \lambda\,(x_{nn} - x_i), \qquad \lambda \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$$

Two fraud points with features (amount z-score, hour-of-day z-score): $x_i = (2.0,\ 3.0)$ and its neighbour $x_{nn} = (4.0,\ 1.0)$.

Difference: $x_{nn} - x_i = (2.0,\ -2.0)$.

Draw $\lambda = 0.3$: $x_{\text{new}} = (2.0 + 0.3 \times 2.0,\ 3.0 + 0.3 \times (-2.0)) = (2.6,\ 2.4)$.

The new point is 30% of the way from $x_i$ to its neighbour. It's plausible only if the space between two fraud cases is also fraud-like, which is the assumption SMOTE quietly makes.

To reach a ratio of 0.2 on our data, SMOTE needs $0.2 \times 9{,}900 - 100 = 1{,}880$ new points, so each real fraud row seeds about 18.8 synthetic ones. With so few real points, those 1,880 all live on the segments between the same 100 rows.

3. SMOTE variants and cleaning methods

Most variants try to fix the "blind to the majority" problem, either by choosing seeds more carefully or by cleaning up afterwards. I'll run each one on the 5 fraud and 12 legit points from the widget, because that toy is deliberately messy and it shows the differences clearly.

  • Borderline-SMOTE only oversamples minority points near the decision boundary (those whose neighbourhoods are mostly majority), on the theory that interior points don't need help.
  • ADASYN generates more synthetic points around minority examples that are harder to learn, weighted by how many majority neighbours each has. It can amplify noise if your "hard" points are mislabelled.
  • Tomek links is a cleaning method: find pairs of opposite-class points that are each other's nearest neighbour, and drop the majority one. It sharpens the boundary a little.
  • ENN (Edited Nearest Neighbours) removes points whose class disagrees with their $k$ neighbours. More aggressive than Tomek. SMOTE+ENN and SMOTE+Tomek combine oversampling with cleaning.
  • SMOTE-NC handles mixed numeric/categorical features by taking the majority category among neighbours instead of interpolating.

Borderline-SMOTE with $m = 5$: a point is "danger" if $m/2 \le n_{\text{legit}} < m$, that is 3 or 4 legit neighbours. All 5 legit means "noise", and fewer than 3 means "safe". A, C and E are danger, so they become the seeds. B and D are written off as noise.

ADASYN with $K = 5$: $r_i = n_{\text{legit}} / 5$. The sum is $0.8 + 1.0 + 0.8 + 1.0 + 0.6 = 4.2$. To make $G = 21$ points, point $i$ gets $g_i = 21 \times r_i / 4.2 = 5 r_i$: that's 4, 5, 4, 5, 3.

The two methods disagree completely about B and D. Borderline-SMOTE ignores them as noise, and ADASYN gives them the most points because they are the hardest. If B and D are mislabelled legit transactions, ADASYN just spent 10 of its 21 points amplifying label errors.

ENN with imbalanced-learn's defaults ($k = 3$, kind_sel="all") removes a legit point if any one of its 3 nearest neighbours is fraud. On this toy, every one of the 12 legit points has a fraud point among its 3 nearest, so all 12 would go. With the classic majority-vote rule (kind_sel="mode"), only the 5 legit points with 2 or 3 fraud neighbours go. Real data is rarely this overlapped, but the strict default can delete far more than you expect.

4. When SMOTE hurts, and how to run it safely

  • High dimensions. Nearest neighbours become nearly meaningless in hundreds of dimensions (the curse of dimensionality), and the segment between two points passes through empty space. Text embeddings and raw pixels are bad candidates.
  • Categorical or one-hot features. Interpolating between "country = FR" and "country = DE" gives a row that's 0.3 French and 0.7 German. Use SMOTE-NC or don't use SMOTE.
  • Overlapping classes. As in the widget, synthetic points land among the majority and teach the model to flag normal behaviour.
  • Strong learners. Several benchmarks (for example Elor and Averbuch-Elor, 2022) found that with tuned gradient boosting and a tuned threshold, SMOTE adds little or nothing over no resampling at all. It helped most for weak learners like plain decision trees.
  • Probabilities. Like all resampling, it shifts the training prior, so raw outputs overestimate the minority class and calibration is gone.

I'm not anti-SMOTE, it just has to beat a weighted baseline with a tuned threshold on your validation folds before I'd keep it. Often it doesn't.

Resample inside the folds

The mistake I see most is running fit_resample on the whole dataset and then cross-validating. Synthetic points built from a fraud row end up in the validation fold while the real row sits in training, and the validation folds are 50:50 instead of 1%. Scores come out wonderful and mean nothing. That's leakage, covered properly in Part 2. The fix is an imbalanced-learn Pipeline, which resamples during fit only and never touches data at predict time.

Code: imbalanced-learn samplers 
from imblearn.under_sampling import RandomUnderSampler, TomekLinks, EditedNearestNeighbours
from imblearn.over_sampling import RandomOverSampler, SMOTE, BorderlineSMOTE, ADASYN, SMOTENC
from imblearn.combine import SMOTETomek, SMOTEENN

# sampling_strategy = desired minority/majority ratio after resampling
rus   = RandomUnderSampler(sampling_strategy=0.1, random_state=0)   # to 10:1
ros   = RandomOverSampler(sampling_strategy=0.2, random_state=0)
smote = SMOTE(sampling_strategy=0.2, k_neighbors=5, random_state=0)
bsm   = BorderlineSMOTE(kind="borderline-1", random_state=0)
ada   = ADASYN(random_state=0)
snc   = SMOTENC(categorical_features=[0, 3, 7], random_state=0)
combo = SMOTEENN(random_state=0)

# Outside a pipeline, only ever on training data:
X_tr_res, y_tr_res = smote.fit_resample(X_train, y_train)
Code: SMOTE inside cross-validation, the safe way 
from imblearn.pipeline import Pipeline          # not sklearn.pipeline
from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedKFold, cross_val_score
from lightgbm import LGBMClassifier

pipe = Pipeline([
    ("smote", SMOTE(sampling_strategy=0.2, random_state=0)),
    ("clf", LGBMClassifier()),
])
cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
# SMOTE is refit on each training fold; validation folds stay at 1% fraud
scores = cross_val_score(pipe, X_train, y_train, cv=cv, scoring="average_precision")

5. Ensembles built for imbalance

Undersampling throws data away. Ensembles get it back by undersampling many times with different random subsets and combining the results, so more of the majority class gets used by some member.

  • EasyEnsemble (Liu, Wu and Zhou, 2009): draw, say, 10 balanced subsets (all 100 fraud + 100 random legit each), train an AdaBoost model on each, average. Each member is cheap to train. The catch is coverage: 10 subsets make 1,000 draws, but only about 956 distinct legit rows. The widget below shows how many subsets you need to see most of them.
  • BalancedRandomForest: a random forest where each tree's bootstrap sample is drawn so the classes are balanced. Same idea at the level of individual trees. A good, hard-to-break baseline for tabular data.
  • RUSBoost and BalancedBagging follow the same pattern with boosting or arbitrary base estimators wrapped in bagging.

One subset draws 100 of 9,900 legit rows, so a given legit row is picked with probability $100 / 9{,}900 = 0.010101$ and missed with probability $0.989899$.

Missed by all 10 subsets: $0.989899^{10} = 0.90346$. Used at least once: $1 - 0.90346 = 0.09654$.

Expected distinct legit rows seen: $9{,}900 \times 0.09654 = 955.7$, about 956. The other 44 of the 1,000 draws are repeats.

Subsets for 90% coverage: solve $0.989899^n = 0.1$, so $n = \ln 0.1 / \ln 0.989899 = -2.302585 / -0.010152 = 226.8$. Round up to 227.

6. Gradient boosting with scale_pos_weight

This one is not an ensemble of resamples, just a weighted loss inside XGBoost or LightGBM. scale_pos_weight multiplies the loss of every positive row. The usual starting value is $\#\text{neg} / \#\text{pos} = 9{,}900/100 = 99$. Tune it: values well below the full ratio (like 10, or $\sqrt{99} \approx 9.95$) often do better on precision-heavy metrics. Also switch the early-stopping metric to aucpr, since the default logloss or AUC will be dominated by the negatives.

On tabular problems my default stack is LightGBM or XGBoost, a moderate scale_pos_weight, early stopping on PR-AUC, then threshold tuning (Part 3). BalancedRandomForest is my second opinion. I rarely end up needing anything fancier. What I don't do is stack corrections: SMOTE to 1:1 plus scale_pos_weight=99 on top flips the imbalance the other way.

Code: imbalanced ensembles and boosting 
from imblearn.ensemble import (
    BalancedRandomForestClassifier, EasyEnsembleClassifier,
    RUSBoostClassifier, BalancedBaggingClassifier,
)

brf = BalancedRandomForestClassifier(
    n_estimators=500, sampling_strategy="all", replacement=True,
    bootstrap=False, random_state=0,
)
eec = EasyEnsembleClassifier(n_estimators=10, random_state=0)  # AdaBoost members

import lightgbm as lgb
lgbm = lgb.LGBMClassifier(
