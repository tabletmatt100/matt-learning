Reinforcement Learning From Scratch: How Agents Learn Without an Answer Key

TL;DR

  • Why standard machine learning isn't enough: In supervised learning, a teacher immediately labels every decision as right or wrong. In reinforcement learning (RL), there is no answer key—an agent takes a chain of actions and only discovers several steps later whether it earned a big reward or failed.
  • The central problem (Credit Assignment): When a warehouse robot reaches a charging dock for +80 points at the end of a hallway, how does it figure out that the quiet early steps (which paid 0 at the time) were actually valuable?
  • The solution (Bootstrapping via Temporal-Difference Learning): Instead of waiting until the end of a long run, each state peeks one step ahead at its neighbor's scorecard $V(S_{t+1})$, discounts it by $\gamma$, and nudges its own score using the surprise (TD error $\delta_t$).

1. The Setup: Why Learning Without a Teacher Is Hard

To see why reinforcement learning exists, compare how you teach a photo classifier versus how you train a game player, a warehouse robot, or a reasoning LLM.

In supervised learning, you hand the model a photo and an exact answer key: "This is a cat." If the model guesses "dog," it knows immediately on that exact step what the right answer was.

Now picture a delivery robot placed in a 3-tile warehouse hallway (Markov Decision Process):

Running Story: The Warehouse Hallway

The robot starts on Tile 1 ($S_0$). At Tile 1, it has two choices (actions $A_0$):

  • Action A (Spare Battery Closet): Step left into a closet that gives +30 power points right now and ends the run.
  • Action B (Walk the Hallway): Step right onto Tile 2 ($R_1 = 0$ points), and from Tile 2 step onto Tile 3 (Main Supercharger), which pays +80 points ($R_2 = +80$).

Notice the catch: when the robot steps from Tile 1 to Tile 2, the environment gives it 0 points. No teacher whispers, "Good job, keep going right!" The robot only sees the +80 jackpot one step later when it reaches Tile 3.

Every interaction in RL follows this four-part loop: the agent observes a state $S_t$, picks an action $A_t$ using its strategy (policy $\pi$), and the environment hands back an immediate reward $R_{t+1}$ plus the new state $S_{t+1}$.

Why can't our warehouse robot on Tile 1 just use standard supervised learning on the immediate feedback it gets after one step?

2. Immediate Bribes vs. Delayed Payoffs: Discounted Return ($G_t$)

If immediate reward $R_{t+1}$ is short-sighted, we should judge a move by the total stream of future rewards it leads to, called the return $G_t$.

Wait: should +80 points two steps in the future count the exact same as +80 points right now? Usually not. Delayed rewards carry risk (the battery might die on the way) and take time. So RL multiplies each step of delay by a discount factor $\gamma$ (gamma) between $0$ and $1$.

For example, if $\gamma = 0.5$, waiting 1 extra step cuts a future reward in half ($\times 0.5$). Waiting 2 extra steps cuts it in half twice ($\times 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25$, or one quarter).

Suppose our robot uses $\gamma = 0.5$ (every extra step of delay cuts a reward in half). If the Main Dock is 2 extra steps away (paying $0$ on step 1, $0$ on step 2, and $+80$ on step 3), which choice has the higher discounted return $G_0$?

Here is the general rule for computing the discounted return $G_t$ from step $t$ onward. Tap any symbol in the box to inspect its role:

$G_t$ $=$ $R_{t+1}$ $+$ $\gamma$ $R_{t+2}$ $+$ $\gamma^2$ $R_{t+3}$ $= $ $R_{t+1}$ $+$ $\gamma$ $G_{t+1}$
Tap any symbol above: $G_t$ = total discounted return | $R_{t+1}$ = immediate reward | $\gamma$ = discount factor | $G_{t+1}$ = return from the next step

Worked Example (with distinct numbers so you can trace every step):

Suppose a 3-step run picks up three different rewards: $R_1 = 2$ on step 1, $R_2 = 6$ on step 2, and $R_3 = 20$ on step 3, with $\gamma = 0.5$. Notice how easy it is to compute $G_t$ by working backward from the final step:

1. Before the final step ($t=2$): only $R_3 = 20$ remains, so $G_2$ $= 20$.
2. One step earlier ($t=1$): $G_1$ $=$ $R_2$ $+$ $0.5$ $\times$ $G_2$ $= 6 + 0.5 \times 20 = 6 + 10 = 16$.
3. At the start ($t=0$): $G_0$ $=$ $R_1$ $+$ $0.5$ $\times$ $G_1$ $= 2 + 0.5 \times 16 = 2 + 8 = 10$.

3. Learning Before the Episode Ends: Bootstrapping & TD Error ($\delta_t$)

In the worked example above, we waited until the robot finished the entire run, then worked backward from the end. That approach is called Monte Carlo (MC) learning.

Waiting until the end has a huge drawback: what if a trip takes 10,000 steps, or never ends at all (like a thermostat managing a server room 24/7)? You could never update your scorecard!

Temporal-Difference (TD) learning fixes this with a clever trick called bootstrapping: update a tile's scorecard after just one step by trusting the neighboring tile's current scorecard.

Instead of waiting for the true final return $G_{t+1}$, the robot plugs in its current estimate of the next tile, $V(S_{t+1})$. The gap between this one-step lookahead (TD target) and what the robot previously expected ($V(S_t)$) is the TD error $\delta_t$:

$\delta_t$ $=$ $\underbrace{R_{t+1} + \gamma V(S_{t+1})}_{\text{One-Step Target}}$ $-$ $\underbrace{V(S_t)}_{\text{Old Estimate}}$
$V_{\text{new}}(S_t)$ $=$ $V(S_t)$ $+$ $\alpha$ $\delta_t$
Tap any symbol: $\delta_t$ = TD surprise | $\alpha$ = learning rate (step size, e.g. $0.5$) | $V(S_t)$ = state scorecard

4. Hands-On Trace: Updating the Scorecard by Hand

Let's trace one TD(0) update using distinct whole numbers so you can see where every number goes, then you complete the final line of a second update.

Full Worked Example ( distinct numbers: $4, 2, 16$ ):
The robot is on Tile 1, which currently has a scorecard estimate of $V(S_1) = 4$. It steps to Tile 2, collects an immediate scrap bonus of $R_2 = 2$, and sees that Tile 2's scorecard is $V(S_2) = 16$. We use $\gamma = 0.5$ and $\alpha = 0.5$.

Step 1 (One-Step Target): Immediate reward plus discounted neighbor value:
$\text{Target} = R_2 + \gamma V(S_2) = 2 + (0.5 \times 16) = 2 + 8 = 10$.

Step 2 (TD Error $\delta_1$): Compare target ($10$) against old estimate ($4$):
$\delta_1 = \text{Target} - V(S_1) = 10 - 4 = +6$ (Tile 1 was underestimated by $6$ points).

Step 3 (Update Scorecard): Move halfway ($\alpha = 0.5$) toward the target:
$V_{\text{new}}(S_1) = V(S_1) + \alpha \delta_1 = 4 + (0.5 \times 6) = 4 + 3 = 7$.

Your Turn: Finish Step 3 (Mental Math)

Update Tile 1 When the Neighbor Is Worth $20$

Now suppose Tile 1 starts at $V(S_1) = 6$, the transition gives immediate reward $R_2 = 4$, and Tile 2 is worth $V(S_2) = 20$ (with $\gamma = 0.5$ and $\alpha = 0.5$):
Step 1 (Target): $4 + (0.5 \times 20) = 4 + 10 = 14$.
Step 2 (TD Error $\delta_1$): $14 - 6 = +8$.
Step 3 (Your turn): Compute $V_{\text{new}}(S_1)$ $= 6 + (0.5 \times 8)$.

Self-Explanation Check

Why Bootstrap Instead of Waiting?

In one sentence, why does TD learning update $V(S_t)$ using the next tile's estimate $V(S_{t+1})$ instead of waiting until the episode ends?

Key idea: By bootstrapping off the neighbor's current scorecard $V(S_{t+1})$, the agent learns immediately after every single move—even in very long or never-ending tasks—without waiting for the run to finish.

5. From Scoring Tiles ($V$) to Picking Moves ($Q$)

There is one piece left in our story. Knowing that "Tile 1 is worth 10 points" ($V(s)$) tells you how good it is to stand on Tile 1, but it does not tell you which button to press (Go Left vs. Go Right) unless you know which move leads where!

To pick actions directly without a map of the warehouse, RL agents keep a slightly more detailed scorecard called the action-value function $Q(s, a)$. Instead of scoring just the tile $s$, $Q(s, a)$ scores each (Tile, Move) pair:

  • $Q(\text{Tile 1}, \text{Left})$ $= 30$ (takes the spare battery closet)
  • $Q(\text{Tile 1}, \text{Right})$ $= 40$ (heads toward the main supercharger at $\gamma = 0.75$)

Once you have $Q(s, a)$ for every move, choosing an action is simple: look at your current tile and pick the move with the highest $Q$ score!

Foundational Method What It Scores One-Step Target When It Works Best
Monte Carlo (MC) $V(s)$ or $Q(s,a)$ Waits for full actual return $G_t$ Short episodes that always end cleanly (e.g., a hand of blackjack)
TD(0) Tile value $V(s)$ $R_{t+1}$ $+$ $\gamma$ $V(S_{t+1})$ Evaluating how good a fixed policy $\pi$ is step-by-step
SARSA (On-Policy) Move value $Q(s,a)$ $R_{t+1}$ $+$ $\gamma$ $Q(S_{t+1}, A_{t+1})$ Cautious control where random exploration mistakes are costly
Q-Learning (Off-Policy) Optimal move $Q^*(s,a)$ $R_{t+1}$ $+$ $\gamma$ $\max_{a'}$ $Q(S_{t+1}, a')$ Learning the best greedy move even while exploring or replaying old logs
