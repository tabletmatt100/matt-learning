Generalized Knowledge Distillation (GKD): Taming Exposure Bias in LLMs

TL;DR

  • The exposure bias trap: Classical knowledge distillation uses teacher forcing (off-policy prefixes $y_{
  • Two GKD levers (Agarwal et al., 2023): Sample a fraction $\lambda \in [0, 1]$ of sequences on-policy from the student itself ($\hat{y} \sim P_{\text{student}}$) and have the teacher score those exact prefixes using a generalized divergence $\mathbb{D}(P_T \,\|\, P_S)$.
  • Why Reverse KL beats Forward KL: Forward KL forces the small student to cover every tail token of the teacher (producing blurry hallucinations), whereas Reverse KL is mode-seeking and severely penalizes any token where the teacher assigns near-zero probability.

1. Why Supervised Distillation Breaks: Exposure Bias

In classical knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015), a small student model $\theta$ matches a large teacher's token distributions on a fixed off-policy dataset $\mathcal{D}$:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^T \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\Big( P_{\text{teacher}}(\cdot \mid x, y_{

During training, the prefix $y_{teacher forcing). At test time, the student must condition on its own sampled tokens $\hat{y}_{exposure bias).

Why does standard supervised distillation struggle on long autoregressive generations?

2. The GKD Framework: Two Levers of Generalization

GKD generalizes distillation along two axes:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{GKD}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, \, y \sim \mu(\cdot \mid x)} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \mathbb{D}\Big( P_{\text{teacher}}(\cdot \mid x, y_{
  1. Sequence Distribution $\mu(\cdot \mid x)$: Mixes static off-policy data with on-policy student rollouts using fraction $\lambda \in [0, 1]$:
    $$\mu(y \mid x) = \lambda P_{\text{student}}(y \mid x) + (1 - \lambda) P_{\text{data}}(y \mid x)$$
    When $\lambda > 0$, the student generates its own trajectory $\hat{y}$, and the teacher gives token-level distribution feedback on the exact states the student visited.
  2. Divergence $\mathbb{D}(P_T \,\|\, P_S)$: Replaces fixed forward KL with Reverse KL or Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) so a smaller student is not forced to spread probability mass over every noisy tail token.

3. Forward KL vs. Reverse KL: Mode-Covering vs. Mode-Seeking

The choice of divergence metric fundamentally changes the student's personality:

Divergence Formula Behavioral Tendency Consequence in Text Generation
Forward KL
$\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(P_T \,\|\, P_S)$		 $\sum_k P_T(k) \ln \frac{P_T(k)}{P_S(k)}$ Mode-Covering (Zero-Avoiding) Forces student to assign $P_S > 0$ anywhere $P_T > 0$. Yields blurry, high-entropy distributions; student hallucinates unlikely tokens.
Reverse KL
$\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(P_S \,\|\, P_T)$		 $\sum_k P_S(k) \ln \frac{P_S(k)}{P_T(k)}$ Mode-Seeking (Zero-Forcing) Heavily punishes student if $P_S > 0$ when $P_T \approx 0$. Forces student to focus exclusively on high-probability modes of the teacher.

4. Fully Worked-Out Numerical Example

Let's trace the exact mathematics of why Reverse KL brutally punishes hallucinations compared to Forward KL.

Consider the single token prediction given prompt: "Today's forecast is..."
Vocabulary: $V = \{\text{"sunny"}, \text{"cloudy"}, \text{"rainy"}\}$.

Given Probabilities

  • Teacher: $P_T = [0.70, 0.25, 0.05]$
  • Student (untrained): $P_S = [0.40, 0.30, 0.30]$

Calculation A: Forward KL $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(P_T \,\|\, P_S)$

$$\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(P_T \,\|\, P_S) = \sum_{k} P_T(k) \ln\left(\frac{P_T(k)}{P_S(k)}\right)$$
  1. For "sunny": $0.70 \times \ln(0.70 / 0.40) = 0.70 \times \ln(1.750) = 0.70 \times 0.5596 = \mathbf{+0.3917}$
  2. For "cloudy": $0.25 \times \ln(0.25 / 0.30) = 0.25 \times \ln(0.8333) = 0.25 \times (-0.1823) = \mathbf{-0.0456}$
  3. For "rainy": $0.05 \times \ln(0.05 / 0.30) = 0.05 \times \ln(0.1667) = 0.05 \times (-1.7918) = \mathbf{-0.0896}$
  4. Total Forward KL: $0.3917 - 0.0456 - 0.0896 = \mathbf{0.2565}$ nats.

Notice that the student allocated 30% probability to "rainy" (when the teacher only gives 5%), yet the penalty from "rainy" in Forward KL is tiny ($-0.0896$) because $P_T(\text{"rainy"})$ is small ($0.05$)! Forward KL barely cares that the student is hallucinating bad tokens.

Calculation B: Reverse KL $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(P_S \,\|\, P_T)$

$$\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(P_S \,\|\, P_T) = \sum_{k} P_S(k) \ln\left(\frac{P_S(k)}{P_T(k)}\right)$$
  1. For "sunny": $0.40 \times \ln(0.40 / 0.70) = 0.40 \times \ln(0.5714) = 0.40 \times (-0.5596) = \mathbf{-0.2238}$
  2. For "cloudy": $0.30 \times \ln(0.30 / 0.25) = 0.30 \times \ln(1.200) = 0.30 \times 0.1823 = \mathbf{+0.0547}$
  3. For "rainy": $0.30 \times \ln(0.30 / 0.05) = 0.30 \times \ln(6.000) = 0.30 \times 1.7918 = \mathbf{+0.5375}$
  4. Total Reverse KL: $-0.2238 + 0.0547 + 0.5375 = \mathbf{0.3684}$ nats.

Look at the difference: For "rainy", Reverse KL produces a massive positive loss penalty of $+0.5375$! Because the weighting term is $P_S(\text{"rainy"}) = 0.30$, any mass the student places where the teacher has low probability is punished aggressively. This forces the student to eliminate hallucinatory tail probabilities.

5. The GKD Training Algorithm: On-Policy Rollouts

How do we practically train with on-policy generation? The GKD training loop proceeds as follows:

1. Sample prompt: $x \sim \mathcal{D}_{\text{prompt}}$
2. With probability $\lambda$: Sample student rollout $y \sim P_{\text{student}}(\cdot \mid x)$
With probability $(1-\lambda)$: Take reference sequence $y \sim \mathcal{D}_{\text{data}}$
3. Run teacher forward pass on sequence $(x, y)$ to obtain $P_{\text{teacher}}(\cdot \mid x, y_{
4. Run student forward pass on sequence $(x, y)$ to obtain $P_{\text{student}}(\cdot \mid x, y_{
5. Compute divergence $\mathbb{D}(P_{\text{teacher}} \,\|\, P_{\text{student}})$ at each generated step $t$
6. Backpropagate gradients into student weights $\theta$

Mathematical Derivation: Policy Gradient for Reverse KL (Click to expand)

When sampling rollouts on-policy $y \sim P_{\text{student}}(\cdot \mid x)$, the gradient with respect to student parameters $\theta$ requires differentiating through the expectation over generated sequences:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{y \sim P_\theta} [ R(y) ] = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim P_\theta} \left[ R(y) \nabla_\theta \log P_\theta(y) \right]$$

In GKD, the token-level loss at step $t$ acts as an intrinsic reward/cost signal from the teacher:

$$r_t = -\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\Big( P_{\text{student}}(\cdot \mid x, y_{

Because the student samples its own trajectory, this gradient directly updates the policy to make transitions into high-teacher-probability trajectories more likely, solving exposure bias completely!
