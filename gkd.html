Generalized Knowledge Distillation (GKD): Taming Exposure Bias in LLMs

Suppose you have a massive 70-billion-parameter teacher LLM and you want to compress its skill into a fast 1-billion-parameter student model. In classic Knowledge Distillation (Hinton et al., 2015), you feed a static dataset of gold responses into both models and train the student to match the teacher's next-token probability distribution at every step.

That works great for image classification, where a model only makes a single prediction. For autoregressive language generation, it hits a wall called exposure bias. During training, the student is always handed the perfect gold prefix $y_{<t}$ from the dataset (teacher forcing). At test time, however, the student has to condition on its own generated words $\hat{y}_{<t}$. The moment the small student picks one slightly unusual word at token 5, it wanders into a prefix it never saw during training, and its errors snowball into hallucinations.

Generalized Knowledge Distillation (GKD) (Agarwal et al., 2023) fixes both parts of that problem: it trains the student on-policy on its own generated prefixes ($\hat{y} \sim P_S$) while asking the teacher to grade every step, and it swaps out mode-covering Forward KL for mode-seeking Reverse KL (or JSD) so the small student locks onto the teacher's best answers instead of smearing probability mass across noisy alternatives.

1. Exposure Bias & On-Policy Student Rollouts ($\lambda$)

Think of learning to drive a car. Teacher forcing (off-policy distillation) is like sitting in the passenger seat watching an expert drive down the dead center of the lane and guessing which way they turn the wheel: you never see what the expert does when the car drifts onto the rumble strips, because the expert never drifts onto the rumble strips!

GKD hands the steering wheel to the student on a fraction $\lambda \in [0, 1]$ of training batches. The student generates its own token sequence $\hat{y} \sim P_S(\cdot \mid x)$, and at every token position $t$ along the student's actual prefix $\hat{y}_{<t}$, the teacher computes its full target vocabulary distribution $P_T(\cdot \mid x, \hat{y}_{<t})$:

$$\mu(y \mid x) = (1 - \lambda)\,P_{\text{data}}(y \mid x) + \lambda\,P_S(y \mid x), \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{GKD}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \mu}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \mathbb{D}\Bigl(P_T(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t}) \;\Big\|\; P_S^\theta(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t})\Bigr)\right]$$

Notice why on-policy GKD ($\lambda = 1$) is so much simpler than standard RLHF (PPO or GRPO): in standard RL, a reward model only gives a single scalar score at the very end of a 500-token response. In On-Policy GKD, the teacher model acts as a dense token-by-token critic on the student's rollouts without needing a separate reward model or value network at all! (Note: because the student's weights $\theta$ only receive gradients through the divergence $\mathbb{D}$ at each token step rather than differentiating through the discrete sampling $y \sim P_S$, GKD avoids high-variance REINFORCE gradients too.)

How does setting the GKD student-rollout fraction to $\lambda = 0.5$ or $1.0$ fix exposure bias compared to standard supervised distillation ($\lambda = 0$)?

2. Forward KL vs. Reverse KL: Mode-Covering vs. Mode-Seeking

The second half of GKD is choosing the divergence $\mathbb{D}(P_T \parallel P_S)$ used to compare the teacher's token distribution $P_T$ against the student's distribution $P_S$ at each position:

$$\underbrace{D_{\text{KL}}(P_T \parallel P_S) = \sum_{v} P_T(v) \log_2 \frac{P_T(v)}{P_S(v)}}_{\text{Forward KL (Mode-Covering)}}, \qquad \underbrace{D_{\text{KL}}(P_S \parallel P_T) = \sum_{v} P_S(v) \log_2 \frac{P_S(v)}{P_T(v)}}_{\text{Reverse KL (Mode-Seeking)}}$$

Why does flipping the order of $P_T$ and $P_S$ completely change the student's behavior when the teacher is multimodal and the small student only has enough capacity to fit one peak?

Look at the denominator inside the logarithm. In Forward KL ($P_T \log \frac{P_T}{P_S}$), whenever the teacher assigns nonzero probability $P_T(v) > 0$ to a token, the student is heavily penalized if $P_S(v) \to 0$. That forces the small student to spread its mass out to cover every plausible phrasing the teacher likes (mode-covering)—often smearing probability into the low-quality valley between two valid phrasings.

In Reverse KL ($P_S \log \frac{P_S}{P_T}$), the teacher $P_T(v)$ sits in the denominator. If the teacher says a token is garbage ($P_T(v) \approx 0$), any student mass $P_S(v) > 0$ blows up $\log \frac{P_S(v)}{P_T(v)} \to \infty$. Conversely, if the student ignores one of the teacher's secondary modes ($P_S(v) = 0$ where $P_T(v) = 0.50$), the term $0 \log(0) = 0$ pays zero penalty! So Reverse KL is mode-seeking: the small student locks cleanly onto one high-confidence teacher mode and never hallucinates low-probability tokens.

Worked Example (Tracing Forward vs. Reverse KL in base-2 bits on a 2-token vocabulary):

Suppose at a phrasing fork, the teacher likes Token A and Token B equally: $P_T = [\frac{1}{2},\; \frac{1}{2}]$. A small student locks decisively onto Token A: $P_S = [\frac{7}{8},\; \frac{1}{8}]$ (or in the clean dyadic limit, $P_S$ puts $\frac{1}{4}$ vs. $\frac{1}{8}$ on a runner-up). Let's compare the penalty on a token $v$ where $P_T(v) = \frac{1}{2}$ vs. a forbidden token $u$ where $P_T(u) = \frac{1}{64}$ ($2^{-6}$):
• If student puts $P_S(u) = \frac{1}{4}$ ($2^{-2}$) on the forbidden token $u$ ($P_T(u) = 2^{-6}$):
Forward KL weights it by $P_T(u) = \frac{1}{64}$: $\frac{1}{64} \log_2(\frac{1/64}{1/4}) = \frac{-4}{64}$ (tiny weight!).
Reverse KL weights it by $P_S(u) = \frac{1}{4}$: $\frac{1}{4} \log_2(\frac{1/4}{1/64}) = \frac{1}{4}(+4\text{ bits}) = \mathbf{+1.0\text{ full bit}}$ of penalty—crushing the hallucination immediately!

A teacher model assigns $P_T(\text{A}) = 0.50$ and $P_T(\text{B}) = 0.50$ to two different valid ways of starting an answer, and $P_T(\text{C}) \approx 0$ to an invalid middle word. A small student lacks the capacity to represent both phrasing paths. Which divergence allows the student to commit $100\%$ to path A without penalty, and which forces it to spread mass across both A and B?

3. Comparing Distillation & Alignment Objectives

Method Prefix Source ($\mu$) Divergence ($\mathbb{D}$) Tradeoff / Personality Best Use Case
Supervised KD (Teacher Forcing) Fixed dataset ($\lambda = 0$) Forward KL $D_{\text{KL}}(P_T \parallel P_S)$ Suffers from exposure bias & mode-covering tail blur Classification or single-token tasks where capacity matches teacher
SeqKD (Hard Teacher Outputs) Teacher rollouts ($y \sim P_T$) Hard token cross-entropy Still off-policy relative to student's own mistakes; drops teacher's soft probabilities Distilling from black-box API teachers that hide logprobs
On-Policy GKD (Reverse KL) Student rollouts ($\lambda \in [0.5, 1]$) Reverse KL $D_{\text{KL}}(P_S \parallel P_T)$ Mode-seeking: eliminates exposure bias and locks onto sharp high-precision teacher modes Math, coding, summarization, and instruction-following student LLMs
On-Policy GKD (Generalized JSD) Mixed rollouts ($\lambda = 0.5$) Bounded $\text{JSD}_\beta(P_T \parallel P_S)$ Smoothly interpolates between mode-covering ($\beta \to 0$) and mode-seeking ($\beta \to 1$) without $\infty$ spikes Stable general-purpose student distillation when $P_T$ has zero-prob tails
