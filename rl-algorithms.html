Modern RL Algorithms: The Story From Q* and PPO to GRPO in LLMs

Every major reinforcement learning algorithm was invented to fix a concrete scaling bottleneck in the one that came before it. When state spaces were small enough to fit in a spreadsheet—like our 3-tile warehouse hallway from Part 1—you could learn an exact lookup table $Q^*(s, a)$ ("Q-star") giving the optimal long-term payoff of every move in every situation (Q-Learning).

The moment you move from small grids to robotic joints or language generation—where a 500-token response has more possible action sequences than atoms in the universe—you can no longer loop over every possible outcome to take a $\max$. You have to train a neural network policy $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ directly with Policy Gradients, nudging up the probability of actions that beat expectations (Advantage $A_t > 0$) while putting a speed limit on each update so a single lucky rollout doesn't destroy the network (PPO).

And when you scale PPO up to 70-billion-parameter reasoning LLMs, keeping a second 70-billion-parameter Critic network in GPU memory just to estimate the baseline expectation becomes painfully expensive. That bottleneck is what led DeepSeek-R1 to GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization): delete the Critic network completely, sample a small group of $G$ answers to the same prompt, and score each answer against the group's own average $\mu$.

1. $Q^*$ ("Q-Star"), Policy Gradients & Why Advantage ($A_t$) Beats Raw Reward

In Part 1, $Q^\pi(s, a)$ estimated the return of taking move $a$ in state $s$ and following the agent's current habit afterward. What if the agent played $100\%$ optimally on every future step? That ultimate scorecard satisfies the Bellman Optimality Equation for $Q^*(s, a)$:

$$Q^*(s, a) = R_{t+1} + \gamma \max_{a'} Q^*(S_{t+1}, a')$$

Why can't we just use Q-Learning to train an LLM's full response? Because computing $\max_{a'} Q(S_{t+1}, a')$ requires checking every possible action $a'$, and when an action is a 1000-token reasoning trace, there are $100{,}000^{1000}$ possible trajectories. Instead, Policy Gradient methods parameterize the policy $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ directly and push up the log-probability $\ln \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)$ of moves that scored well.

Here is the catch: if an easy math prompt has three candidate answers that score $+90$, $+95$, and $+100$ points, multiplying $\nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta$ by the raw reward pushes up the probability of all three answers—including the worst $+90$ one! In Actor-Critic RL, a Critic network predicts the expected baseline score $V(s_t) = 95$, and the Actor updates using the centered Advantage $A_t = R - V(s_t)$: now $+100$ gets a positive push ($A = +5$), while $+90$ gets pushed down ($A = -5$).

From Room 1, pressing the Blue Button pays immediate reward $R = 4$ and opens Room 2. Inside Room 2, Lever A has optimal value $Q^*(\text{Room 2}, \text{A}) = 6$ and Lever B has $Q^*(\text{Room 2}, \text{B}) = 10$. With discount $\gamma = 0.5$, what is $Q^*(\text{Room 1}, \text{Blue})$?

2. PPO's Speed Limit ($r_t$) & How GRPO Eliminates the Critic Network

Even with centered Advantages $A_t$, standard policy gradients have a fatal habit: if a batch produces a huge gradient step that triples a token's probability in one update, the LLM's language distribution can collapse into gibberish—and because the collapsed policy now generates terrible rollouts on the next batch, it never recovers.

PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) prevents policy collapse by tracking the probability ratio $r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t \mid s_t)}$ between the new policy and the policy that collected the rollout. Whenever a positive-advantage move ($A_t > 0$) tries to grow past $1 + \epsilon$ (typically $1.20$ for $\epsilon = 0.20$), or a negative-advantage move ($A_t < 0$) tries to shrink below $1 - \epsilon = 0.80$, PPO's clipped objective flattens the gradient to zero—acting as a hard per-step speed limit.

While PPO works well, running it on a 70B LLM requires holding a separate 70B Critic network $V_\phi(s)$ (plus its AdamW optimizer states) in VRAM just to compute the baseline in $A_t = R - V_\phi(s)$. GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) deletes the Critic network entirely: for each prompt $x$, it samples a group of $G$ candidate responses ($y_1, \dots, y_G$), scores them with a verifier ($R_1, \dots, R_G$), and normalizes each response's reward against the group's own mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$:

$$r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t \mid s_t)}, \qquad A_i^{\text{GRPO}} = \frac{R_i - \mu}{\sigma}, \quad \text{where } \mu = \frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^G R_j$$

Worked Example (Computing GRPO group-relative advantages by hand for $G = 3$ responses):

For one coding prompt, GRPO samples $G = 3$ candidate programs that receive rewards $R_1 = 1,\; R_2 = 4,\; R_3 = 10$ (with group spread $\sigma = 3$):
1. Group Mean ($\mu$): $\mu = \frac{1 + 4 + 10}{3} = \frac{15}{3} = \mathbf{5}$ (replaces the Critic network's baseline!).
2. Group-Relative Advantages ($A_i = (R_i - \mu)/\sigma$):
• Response 1: $A_1 = \frac{1 - 5}{3} = \mathbf{-\frac{4}{3}}$ (below group average $\to$ probability pushed down).
• Response 2: $A_2 = \frac{4 - 5}{3} = \mathbf{-\frac{1}{3}}$ (slightly below average $\to$ nudged down).
• Response 3: $A_3 = \frac{10 - 5}{3} = \mathbf{+\frac{5}{3}}$ (beat the group average $\to$ reinforced!).

During a PPO update with clip range $\epsilon = 0.20$ (so the allowed ratio band is $[0.80, 1.20]$), an action with positive advantage $A_t = +5$ had old probability $\pi_{\text{old}} = 0.10$ and has already risen to new probability $\pi_\theta = 0.15$.
