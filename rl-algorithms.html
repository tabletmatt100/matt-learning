Modern RL Algorithms: The Story From Q* and PPO to GRPO in LLMs

TL;DR

  • The overarching story: Every famous RL algorithm was invented to fix a concrete bottleneck in the previous one—moving from small grid games ($Q^*$ and Q-Learning) to huge continuous spaces (Policy Gradients and PPO) to memory-efficient LLM reasoning (GRPO).
  • $Q^*(s, a)$ ("Q-star"): The ultimate action cheat sheet—the expected return of taking move $a$ in state $s$ assuming you play perfectly on every step after. Great when you can check all possible actions, or score candidate reasoning steps in a search tree.
  • PPO vs. GRPO (for training LLMs): PPO keeps a separate, giant Critic neural network to judge whether an answer beat expectations (Advantage $A_t$) and clips updates so the model doesn't collapse. GRPO (used in DeepSeek-R1) deletes the Critic network entirely: it generates a small group of $G$ answers to the same prompt and uses their own group average $\mu$ as the baseline.

1. Chapter 1: $Q^*$ ("Q-Star"), Q-Learning, and Why Lookup Tables Break

In Part 1, our warehouse robot learned a scorecard $Q(s, a)$ that estimates how many total points it will get if it takes move $a$ in state $s$ and follows its current habit afterward.

What if the robot stopped making beginner mistakes on future turns and played 100% optimally from the very next step onward? That ultimate scorecard is called $Q^*(s, a)$ ("Q-star").

If somebody hands you $Q^*(s, a)$, you never have to plan ahead again. At any state $s$, you just look at the $Q^*$ numbers for your available moves and pick the biggest one ($\arg\max_a Q^*(s, a)$).

$Q^*(s, a)$ $=$ $R_{t+1}$ $+$ $\gamma$ $\max_{a'}$ $Q^*(S_{t+1}, a')$
Bellman Optimality Rule (tap any symbol): Immediate reward $R_{t+1}$ + discounted score of the BEST move $a'$ at the next state $S_{t+1}$

Worked Example (Tracing $Q^*$ with distinct numbers):

From Room 1, pressing the Blue Button gives an immediate $R = 4$ points and opens Room 2. Inside Room 2, there are two levers with known optimal scores:

  • Lever A has $Q^*(\text{Room 2}, \text{Lever A}) = 6$
  • Lever B has $Q^*(\text{Room 2}, \text{Lever B}) = 10$

With discount factor $\gamma = 0.5$, how much is pressing the Blue Button in Room 1 worth under $Q^*$?
1. Pick the best future move in Room 2: $\max(6, 10)$ $= 10$.
2. Discount it by one step ($\gamma = 0.5$): $0.5 \times 10 = 5$.
3. Add the immediate reward ($R = 4$): $Q^*(\text{Room 1}, \text{Blue})$ $= 4 + 5 = 9$.

Two Things People Mean by "Q*"
  • 1. In textbook RL: $Q^*(s, a)$ is the optimal action-value function above, learned by Q-Learning (in a table) or DQN (Deep Q-Network) (using a neural network when states are images or continuous numbers).
  • 2. In LLM reasoning news: "Q*" also became famous as a shorthand for combining a learned step-verifier ($Q^*$) with tree search (like $A^*$ search). Instead of generating a math proof blind token-by-token, the model scores candidate intermediate steps with a $Q^*$ value model so it can backtrack out of dead-end reasoning steps early.

Taking Action X in State 1 pays an immediate reward of $R = 3$ and lands in State 2. State 2 has three possible moves with $Q^*$ values of $4$, $8$, and $14$. If $\gamma = 0.5$, what is $Q^*(\text{State 1}, \text{Action X})$?

2. Chapter 2: Policy Gradients & Actor-Critic (Why "Advantage" Beats Raw Reward)

If Q-Learning and DQN work so well for video games, why don't we train Large Language Models (LLMs) purely with Q-Learning?

Look at the $\max_{a'} Q(S_{t+1}, a')$ step in the equation above. In Pac-Man, there are only 4 joystick actions (Up, Down, Left, Right), so checking all 4 to find the max is instant. An LLM writing a 300-word response chooses from 100,000+ vocabulary tokens at every step—you cannot loop over every possible action to compute a maximum!

So for LLMs and continuous robots, we flip the design around. Instead of learning a scorecard first and taking a max, Policy Gradient methods train a neural network called the Actor $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ that outputs action probabilities directly:

  • Sample an action (or a full response) from $\pi_\theta$.
  • If it scored better than expected, turn up its probability.
  • If it scored worse than expected, turn down its probability.
Guess First: The All-Positive Reward Trap

Should We Reinforce an Answer That Scored +6?

Suppose you ask an LLM a very easy prompt ("What is $2 + 2$?"). Almost any answer scores positively: a sloppy rambling answer gets $R = +6$, while a crisp answer gets $R = +10$, and the average expected score for this easy prompt is $V(s) = +8$. If the LLM outputs the sloppy answer and gets +6, what happens if we reinforce every positive reward?

To avoid rewarding mediocre answers on easy prompts (or over-punishing decent answers on brutal prompts), Actor-Critic algorithms subtract the prompt's expected baseline score $V(s)$ from the reward. The difference is called the Advantage $A_t$:

$A_t$ $=$ $\underbrace{R}_{\text{Actual Score}}$ $-$ $\underbrace{V(s)}_{\text{Critic's Expected Baseline}}$
Tap any symbol: $A_t > 0$ means better than usual (reinforce!) | $A_t < 0$ means worse than usual (suppress!)

In classic Actor-Critic, two neural networks train side by side:

  • The Actor $\pi_\theta$: Generates the actions (or text) and updates its probabilities using $A_t$.
  • The Critic $V_\phi(s)$: Predicts the expected baseline score $V(s)$ for each state/prompt so the Actor knows what "average" looks like.

3. Chapter 3: PPO — Putting a Speed Limit on Policy Updates

Even with Advantage $A_t$, raw Policy Gradient training has a dangerous failure mode: policy collapse.

Suppose an LLM happens to sample one lucky response with a huge advantage ($A_t = +10$). If you take a large gradient step to crank up those token probabilities, you can overshoot and scramble the neural network's weights. Worse yet, once an RL agent scrambles its own policy, it generates garbage trajectories on the next batch, learns from that garbage, and never recovers.

PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization)—the default RL algorithm behind classic RLHF in ChatGPT—fixes this by putting a strict speed limit on how far the new policy $\pi_{\text{new}}$ is allowed to drift from the old policy $\pi_{\text{old}}$ in a single update.

First, PPO computes the probability ratio $r_t$:

$r_t$ $=$ $\dfrac{\text{New Probability}}{\text{Old Probability}}$ $=$ $\dfrac{\pi_{\text{new}}(a \mid s)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a \mid s)}$
Example: If old probability was $10\%$ and new probability is $15\%$, then $r_t = 15 / 10 = 1.5$.

Next, PPO clips $r_t$ inside a narrow safety corridor $[1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon]$. With the standard $\epsilon = 0.2$, the ratio is fenced between $0.8$ and $1.2$ ($\pm 20\%$):

  • If $A_t > 0$ (good move), PPO stops rewarding you for pushing $r_t$ above $1.2$.
  • If $A_t < 0$ (bad move), PPO stops rewarding you for pushing $r_t$ below $0.8$.

4. Chapter 4: GRPO — How DeepSeek Eliminated the Critic Network

PPO is stable, so why did LLM researchers look for a replacement when training massive reasoning models like DeepSeekMath and DeepSeek-R1?

Count the neural networks sitting in GPU memory during PPO:

  1. The Actor LLM being trained (say, 70 billion parameters).
  2. And the Critic value network $V_\phi(s)$ (another 70 billion parameters!) whose entire job is just predicting the baseline score so we can compute Advantage $A_t = R - V(s)$.

Keeping and training that second 70-billion-parameter Critic network nearly doubles memory and compute cost. Even worse, when an LLM writes a 2,000-token chain-of-thought math proof and only gets a score at the very last token (right or wrong answer), training a Critic to accurately guess the value of token #437 is slow and noisy.

The GRPO Breakthrough (Group Relative Policy Optimization)

Ask yourself: why did we need the Critic in the first place? Only to answer one question: "What is the average baseline score for this prompt?"

GRPO replaces the entire Critic neural network with a simple group average: for a given prompt, generate a small group of $G$ candidate responses (for example, $4$ responses), score all $4$, and use the average of those $4$ scores ($\mu$) as your baseline!

$A_i$ $=$ $\dfrac{\text{Answer } i \text{'s Score} - \text{Group Average}}{\text{Group Spread}}$ $=$ $\dfrac{R_i - \mu}{\sigma}$
Tap any symbol: $R_i$ = reward of response $i$ | $\mu$ = mean of the $G$ responses | $\sigma$ = standard deviation (spread) of the $G$ responses

Worked Example (Tracing GRPO across $G = 4$ distinct answers—zero calculator needed):

We give one math prompt to our LLM and sample $G = 4$ candidate responses. The verifier scores them with four distinct numbers:

Response 1: $R_1 = 2$  |  Response 2: $R_2 = 4$  |  Response 3: $R_3 = 6$  |  Response 4: $R_4 = 12$

Step 1 (Group Average $\mu$): Add the 4 scores and divide by 4:
$\mu = (2 + 4 + 6 + 12) \div 4 = 24 \div 4 = 6$.

Step 2 (Subtract the Baseline $\mu = 6$ from each score):
• Response 1: $2 - 6 = -4$ (well below group average → suppress)
• Response 2: $4 - 6 = -2$ (slightly below average → suppress)
• Response 3: $6 - 6 = 0$ (exactly average → neutral)
• Response 4: $12 - 6 = +6$ (beat the group average → reinforce!)

Step 3 (Scale by spread, say $\sigma = 2$): Divide each difference by $2$:
$A_1 = -2$,  $A_2 = -1$,  $A_3 = 0$,  $A_4 = +3$.

How Does GRPO Save Half the Training Memory of PPO?

In one sentence, what neural network does GRPO remove compared to PPO, and what replaces it?

Key idea: GRPO deletes the giant Critic value network $V_\phi(s)$ and replaces its baseline prediction with the empirical average score $\mu$ of a small group of $G$ responses sampled for the same prompt.

5. Chapter 5: The Algorithm Map ($Q^*$, PPO, DPO, GRPO) & Checks

You will often see one more acronym alongside PPO and GRPO in LLM papers: DPO (Direct Preference Optimization).

Where does DPO fit? Unlike PPO and GRPO—which are online RL methods that actively generate new responses during training and score them—DPO is an offline shortcut. If you already have a static dataset of human preference pairs (winning answer, losing answer), DPO directly nudges the LLM to raise the probability of the winning answer and lower the losing one without generating new rollouts. That makes DPO cheap and simple for style alignment, whereas online RL (GRPO / PPO) shines when an LLM needs to practice and discover new multi-step math or code solutions.

Algorithm Core Mechanism Needs a Critic Network? Best Use Case
Q-Learning / DQN ($Q^*$) Learns optimal action scorecard $Q^*(s,a)$ via $\max_{a'} Q$ Only a Q-network (no separate Actor) Games, robotics grids, or scoring discrete steps in a search tree
PPO Clips policy ratio $r_t \in [1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$ using learned baseline $V(s)$ Yes (full-size Critic network) Continuous robot control & general RLHF when memory is plentiful
DPO Trains directly on offline (chosen, rejected) pairs; no live rollouts No (no Critic, no online rollouts) Fast, low-cost chat style & preference tuning on static datasets
GRPO Samples $G$ answers per prompt; uses group mean $\mu$ as baseline + PPO clipping No (group mean replaces Critic!) Training reasoning LLMs (math, code, logic) with verifiable rewards
