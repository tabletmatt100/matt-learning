Calculus for ML Refresher: The 10% of Calc 1–3 You Actually Need

If you took Calculus 1, 2, and 3 in school, you probably spent 80% of your time on trig substitutions, integration by parts, convergence tests for infinite series, and triple integrals in spherical coordinates. When you open a deep learning paper or write PyTorch code, none of that shows up.

Machine learning uses almost exclusively differential calculus (derivatives), and only a narrow slice of it: taking partial derivatives by treating every other variable as a constant, stacking those partials into a gradient vector $\nabla_w L$ that points steepest uphill, multiplying local slopes along nested functions via the Chain Rule (backpropagation), and using second derivatives (curvature) to understand why large learning rates overshoot and explode.

1. Partial Derivatives & The Gradient Vector ($\nabla_w L$)

In single-variable Calc 1, the derivative $f'(w)$ is the local slope of a curve: if $f'(w) = +4$, nudging $w$ up by $+0.01$ changes $f(w)$ by roughly $(+4)(0.01) = +0.04$.

In multivariable Calc 3, a neural network's loss $L(w_1, w_2, \dots, w_d)$ depends on millions of weights at once. Taking a partial derivative $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1}$ requires zero new math: you simply pretend every other weight ($w_2, w_3, \dots$) is a frozen number and take the ordinary Calc 1 derivative with respect to $w_1$. Stacking all $d$ partial derivatives into a vector gives the gradient $\nabla_w L$:

$$\nabla_w L = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad w_{\text{new}} = w - \eta \nabla_w L$$

Why does $\nabla_w L$ always point in the direction of steepest loss increase? Because for any small unit step direction $u$, the rate of change of $L$ (directional derivative) is the dot product $\nabla_w L \cdot u = \|\nabla_w L\|_2 \cos\theta$. Since $\cos\theta$ peaks at $+1$ when $\theta = 0^\circ$ ($u$ aligned with $\nabla_w L$) and hits $-1$ when $\theta = 180^\circ$ ($u$ opposite $\nabla_w L$), stepping in $-\eta \nabla_w L$ is guaranteed to point steepest downhill locally.

Worked Example (One step of 2D gradient descent by hand):

Suppose our loss surface is the bowl $L(w_1, w_2) = w_1^2 + 3w_2^2$, and we start at $(w_1, w_2) = (2, -1)$ (initial loss $= 2^2 + 3(-1)^2 = \mathbf{7}$) with learning rate $\eta = 0.1$:
1. Partial derivatives: Freezing $w_2$, $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} = 2w_1 = 2(2) = \mathbf{+4}$. Freezing $w_1$, $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2} = 6w_2 = 6(-1) = \mathbf{-6}$. So $\nabla_w L = [4,\; -6]^\top$.
2. Step downhill ($w - 0.1\nabla_w L$): $w_{\text{new}} = [2 - 0.1(4),\; -1 - 0.1(-6)]^\top = [\mathbf{1.6},\; \mathbf{-0.4}]^\top$.
3. New loss: $L(1.6, -0.4) = (1.6)^2 + 3(-0.4)^2 = 2.56 + 0.48 = \mathbf{3.04}$ (cut the loss more than in half in a single step!).

Let $L(w_1, w_2) = 2w_1^2 + w_1 w_2$. At the point $(w_1, w_2) = (1, 3)$, what is the partial derivative $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1}$?

2. The Chain Rule, Second Derivatives (Curvature) & The 4 Essential Slopes

A neural network is just nested functions: weight $w$ multiplies input $x$ to make logit $z = wx + b$, which passes through an activation to make prediction $\hat{y} = f(z)$, which enters loss $L(\hat{y}, y)$. The Chain Rule states that sensitivity along a serial chain is the product of the local slopes (and if one variable branches into two paths, you add the two path products):

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{y}} \cdot \frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial z} \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial w}, \qquad L(w + \Delta w) \approx L(w) + g\,\Delta w + \frac{1}{2} H\,(\Delta w)^2$$

Look at the second equation (second-order Taylor approximation), where $g = L'(w)$ is the first derivative (slope) and $H = L''(w)$ is the second derivative (curvature / Hessian). The first derivative $g$ tells you which way is downhill right now; the second derivative $H$ tells you how rapidly that slope changes as you move. If $H$ is huge (a steep, narrow canyon) and your learning rate $\eta > 2 / H$, a downhill step jumps completely across the valley floor and lands higher up the opposite wall!

A neuron computes $z = w \cdot x$ with $x = 3$ and $w = 2$ (so $z = 6$), passes $z$ through an active ReLU $\hat{y} = \max(0, z) = 6$ (local slope $= 1$), and evaluates squared loss $L = \frac{1}{2}(\hat{y} - y)^2$ against target $y = 2$. What is $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}$?

At the output of a classifier, logits $z$ pass through softmax $\hat{p} = \text{softmax}(z)$ into cross-entropy loss $L = -\ln \hat{p}_{\text{true}}$. If the true class is class 1 ($y_1 = 1$) and the model predicts probability $\hat{p}_1 = 0.75$, what does the combined derivative $\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_1}$ simplify to?

3. The Only 4 Derivatives You Need to Know by Heart in ML

Function $f(z)$ Derivative $f'(z)$ Why It Matters in Deep Learning
Quadratic Bowl: $\frac{1}{2}(z - y)^2$ $z - y$ (and $f'' = 1$) Slope is proportional to error; shrinks smoothly to $0$ as $z \to y$ (MSE & $L_2$ decay)
ReLU: $\max(0, z)$ $1$ if $z > 0$, else $0$ Slope $= 1$ passes gradients across 100 layers without shrinking; slope $= 0$ causes dead neurons
Sigmoid: $\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$ $\sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z)) \le 0.25$ Max slope is only $\frac{1}{4}$ at $z=0$ and $\approx 0$ in tails, causing vanishing gradients in deep stacks
Softmax + Cross-Entropy: $-\sum y_k \ln \hat{p}_k$ $\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i} = \hat{p}_i - y_i$ Logarithm undoes softmax's exponential so the logit gradient is simply $(\text{prediction} - \text{truth})$
