LoRA & QLoRA From Scratch: Low-Rank Adaptation Without Full Fine-Tuning

Suppose you want to fine-tune a 7-billion-parameter language model on your own dataset. Loading the model's 16-bit weights for inference only takes $14\text{ GB}$ of GPU memory. The moment you run standard AdamW fine-tuning across all 7 billion parameters, however, your VRAM bill explodes past $60\text{ to }80\text{ GB}$—because for every trainable weight, AdamW has to store a gradient plus two 32-bit running averages (momentum and variance) alongside a 32-bit master weight copy.

Do we really need to update all $16.7\text{ million}$ numbers inside a $4096 \times 4096$ weight matrix just to teach an already-fluent model a new domain or output format? In 2021, Hu et al. showed that while pretraining from scratch requires full high-rank matrices, adapting a pretrained model happens inside a tiny, low-dimensional subspace.

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) exploits that discovery directly: it freezes the huge pretrained matrix $W_0$ completely (eliminating $100\%$ of its optimizer state) and routes a parallel update $\Delta W = B A$ through two skinny matrices of rank $r \ll d$ (say, $r = 8$ or $16$). During training, you update less than $1\%$ of the parameters; after training, because $W_0 x + B A x = (W_0 + B A)x$ is purely linear, you simply add $B A$ directly into $W_0$ once and deploy the model with zero extra inference latency.

1. The Initialization Puzzle: How Do You Start at $\Delta W = 0$ Without Locking Gradients?

In LoRA, we attach a parallel low-rank bypass $\Delta W = B A$ beside a frozen pretrained layer $W_0$:

$$h = W_0 x + \Delta W x = W_0 x + \frac{\alpha}{r} B(Ax)$$

At training step $0$, we need $\Delta W = B A = 0$ so the network starts out producing the exact same outputs as the pretrained base model without any initial random shock. Why not just initialize both $A = 0$ and $B = 0$?

2. Tracing a $3 \times 3$ Low-Rank Update & Zero-Latency Merging

A dense matrix $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ has $d \cdot k$ entries. A rank-$r$ LoRA update factors $\Delta W = B A$ into a tall matrix $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and a wide matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$, storing only $r(d + k)$ numbers.

Worked $3 \times 3$ rank-$1$ trace with distinct numbers ($\alpha = 1, r = 1$):

Suppose our frozen pretrained matrix is $W_0$ $(3 \times 3)$, our rank-$1$ adapter has column $B$ $(3 \times 1)$ and row $A$ $(1 \times 3)$, and our input vector is $x = [1, 2, 1]^\top$:

$$W_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad x = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Route 1: Two-branch training forward pass ($W_0 x + B(Ax)$):
1. Frozen base output: $W_0 x = [2(1)+0(2)+1(1),\; 1(1)+3(2)+0(1),\; 0(1)+1(2)+4(1)]^\top = [\mathbf{3}, \mathbf{7}, \mathbf{6}]^\top$.
2. Down-project to $r=1$ bottleneck scalar: $u = A x = 2(1) + 1(2) + 3(1) = \mathbf{7}$.
3. Up-project back to $3$ dims: $B u = [1, -1, 2]^\top \cdot 7 = [\mathbf{7}, \mathbf{-7}, \mathbf{14}]^\top$.
4. Sum branches: $h = W_0 x + B(Ax) = [3+7,\; 7-7,\; 6+14]^\top = [\mathbf{10}, \mathbf{0}, \mathbf{20}]^\top$.

Route 2: Merged single-matrix inference pass ($W_{\text{merged}} x$):
Because matrix multiplication is associative and distributive, $W_0 x + B A x = (W_0 + BA)x$. We form the outer product $\Delta W = B A$ once after training finishes:

$$\Delta W = B A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 3 \\ -2 & -1 & -3 \\ 4 & 2 & 6 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad W_{\text{merged}} = W_0 + \Delta W = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{4} & \mathbf{1} & \mathbf{4} \\ \mathbf{-1} & \mathbf{2} & \mathbf{-3} \\ \mathbf{4} & \mathbf{3} & \mathbf{10} \end{bmatrix}$$

Multiply $W_{\text{merged}} x$ directly: $[4(1)+1(2)+4(1),\; -1(1)+2(2)-3(1),\; 4(1)+3(2)+10(1)]^\top = [\mathbf{10}, \mathbf{0}, \mathbf{20}]^\top$. Exact match, with zero extra matrices or latency at deployment! (And to switch tasks, you subtract $B_1 A_1$ and add $B_2 A_2$ in milliseconds.)

3. Parameter & VRAM compression across ranks

Why does freezing $W_0$ save so much GPU memory? For every trainable parameter, AdamW stores the BF16 gradient ($2\text{ B}$) plus FP32 master weights, momentum $m$, and variance $v$ ($12\text{ B}$) — $14\text{ bytes}$ of dynamic state on top of the $2\text{ B}$ weight itself. A frozen weight needs $0\text{ bytes}$ of gradient or optimizer state.

$$\text{Full matrix params} = d \cdot k, \qquad \text{LoRA params} = r(d + k), \qquad \text{Compression ratio} = \frac{d \cdot k}{r(d + k)}$$
For a square matrix $d = k = 4096$: $\text{Compression ratio} = \frac{4096^2}{r(8192)} = \frac{2048}{r}$. At $r = 8$, LoRA trains $\mathbf{256\times}$ fewer weights ($65{,}536$ vs. $16{,}777{,}216$).

4. Why $\alpha / r$ Exists, Which Matrices to Adapt, and How QLoRA Works

Why does the LoRA formula multiply $B A x$ by the constant $\frac{\alpha}{r}$? Suppose you train a model at rank $r = 8$ and then decide to try $r = 16$. Because the inner product $B A x = \sum_{i=1}^{r} b_{\cdot i}(a_{i\cdot} x)$ adds together $r$ rank-1 columns, doubling $r$ doubles the magnitude of the sum and its gradients. Dividing explicitly by $r$ normalizes that growth so you can sweep across $r = 8, 16, 64$ without having to retune your learning rate $\eta$ or $\alpha$.

Which weight matrices inside a Transformer should actually get LoRA adapters? While the original 2021 paper only attached adapters to the attention query and value projections ($W_q, W_v$), Dettmers et al. (2023) showed that adapting every linear layer in the block—all four attention projections ($W_q, W_k, W_v, W_o$) plus all three feed-forward projections ($W_{\text{gate}}, W_{\text{up}}, W_{\text{down}}$)—at a modest rank like $r = 16$ consistently outperforms adapting only $W_q, W_v$ even at a huge rank like $r = 64$.

To squeeze that entire training run onto a single consumer GPU, QLoRA compresses the frozen base weights $W_0$ from 16-bit down to 4-bit NormalFloat4 (NF4) with double quantization ($\approx 0.55\text{ bytes/weight}$, shrinking a 7B model's base weights from $14\text{ GB}$ to $\sim 3.9\text{ GB}$). During each forward and backward step, QLoRA dequantizes blocks of $W_0$ on the fly into 16-bit BF16 registers to compute the matrix products, while accumulating gradients exclusively into the 16-bit LoRA adapters $A$ and $B$.

Why LoRA still needs activation memory (and why gradient checkpointing still matters)

A common surprise is that freezing $99\%$ of a model's weights eliminates $99\%$ of optimizer VRAM, yet long-sequence training can still run out of memory.

Look at the backward rule for the LoRA adapter in layer $\ell$: to compute $\bar{B}_\ell = \bar{h}_\ell (A_\ell x_\ell)^\top$ and $\bar{A}_\ell = B_\ell^\top \bar{h}_\ell x_\ell^\top$, and to propagate $\bar{x}_\ell = (W_{0,\ell} + \frac{\alpha}{r}B_\ell A_\ell)^\top \bar{h}_\ell$ backward from layer $L$ down to layer $1$, the network must still cache the intermediate forward activations $x_\ell$ across every layer! Therefore, for long context lengths ($4096+$ tokens), you still pair LoRA/QLoRA with activation checkpointing.
