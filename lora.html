LoRA & QLoRA From Scratch: Low-Rank Adaptation Without Full Fine-Tuning

Suppose you want to fine-tune a 7-billion-parameter language model on your own dataset. Loading the model's 16-bit weights for inference only takes $14\text{ GB}$ of GPU memory. The moment you run standard AdamW fine-tuning across all 7 billion parameters, however, your VRAM bill explodes past $60\text{ to }80\text{ GB}$—because for every trainable weight, AdamW has to store a gradient plus two 32-bit running averages (momentum $m$ and variance $v$) alongside a 32-bit master weight copy.

Do we really need to update all $16.7\text{ million}$ numbers inside a $4096 \times 4096$ weight matrix just to teach an already-fluent model a new domain or output format? In 2021, Hu et al. showed that while pre-training from scratch requires full high-rank matrices, adapting an already pre-trained model happens inside a tiny, low-dimensional subspace.

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) exploits that discovery directly: it freezes the huge pre-trained matrix $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ completely (eliminating $100\%$ of its optimizer state) and routes a parallel update $\Delta W = B A$ through two skinny matrices $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$ of rank $r \ll \min(d, k)$ (say, $r = 8$ or $16$). During training, you update less than $1\%$ of the parameters; after training, because $W_0 x + B A x = (W_0 + B A)x$ is purely linear, you simply add $B A$ directly into $W_0$ once and deploy the model with zero extra inference latency.

1. The Asymmetric Initialization Rule & Zero-Latency Merging

In LoRA, we attach a parallel low-rank bypass beside a frozen pre-trained layer $W_0$:

$$h = W_0 x + \Delta W x = W_0 x + \frac{\alpha}{r} B(Ax)$$

At training step $0$, we want $\Delta W = B A = 0$ so the network starts out producing the exact same outputs as the pre-trained base model without any random shock. Why not just initialize both $A = 0$ and $B = 0$?

Because of the multiply-gate partner-swap rule in backpropagation! The gradient into $B$ multiplies by $A x$ ($\bar{B} \propto \bar{h}(Ax)^\top$), and the gradient into $A$ multiplies by $B^\top \bar{h}$ ($\bar{A} \propto B^\top \bar{h} x^\top$). If both $A = 0$ and $B = 0$ at step 0, then $\bar{B} = 0$ and $\bar{A} = 0$ forever—a total deadlock. Instead, LoRA initializes $A \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ with random Gaussian weights and sets $B = 0$. At step 0, the product $B A$ is still $0$, so the model output is unchanged; on the very first backward pass, the nonzero $A x$ gives $\bar{B} \ne 0$, waking $B$ up on step 1 and unlocking $A$ on step 2.

Worked Example (Tracing a $3 \times 3$ rank-$1$ LoRA update & offline merge with $\alpha = 1, r = 1$):

Take a frozen $3 \times 3$ matrix $W_0$, a rank-$1$ adapter with column $B$ ($3 \times 1$) and row $A$ ($1 \times 3$), and input $x = [1, 2, 1]^\top$:

$$W_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad x = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

1. Two-branch training pass ($W_0 x + B(Ax)$): Frozen base gives $W_0 x = [3, 7, 6]^\top$. The bottleneck scalar is $u = A x = 2(1) + 1(2) + 3(1) = \mathbf{7}$, so $B u = [7, -7, 14]^\top$. Summing the two branches gives $h = [3+7,\; 7-7,\; 6+14]^\top = [\mathbf{10}, \mathbf{0}, \mathbf{20}]^\top$.
2. Merged single-matrix inference pass ($(W_0 + BA)x$): After training, form the outer product $\Delta W = B A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 3 \\ -2 & -1 & -3 \\ 4 & 2 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$ and add it once to $W_0$ to get $W_{\text{merged}} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 & 4 \\ -1 & 2 & -3 \\ 4 & 3 & 10 \end{bmatrix}$. Multiplying $W_{\text{merged}} x$ directly gives $[4+2+4,\; -1+4-3,\; 4+6+10]^\top = [\mathbf{10}, \mathbf{0}, \mathbf{20}]^\top$—exact match with zero extra matrices at deployment!

An older Houlsby bottleneck adapter computes $h = W_0 x + B\,\text{ReLU}(A x)$, whereas LoRA computes $h = W_0 x + \frac{\alpha}{r} B (A x)$ with no nonlinearity between $A$ and $B$. Why does omitting the ReLU between $A$ and $B$ matter at deployment?

2. Why $\alpha / r$ Exists, Which Layers to Target, and How QLoRA Works

Why does the LoRA formula multiply $B A x$ by the constant $\frac{\alpha}{r}$? Suppose you train a model at rank $r = 8$ and then decide to test $r = 32$. Because the inner product $B A x = \sum_{i=1}^{r} b_{\cdot i}(a_{i\cdot} x)$ adds together $r$ rank-1 columns, quadrupling $r$ quadruples the magnitude of the sum and its gradients. Dividing explicitly by $r$ normalizes that growth so you can sweep across $r = 8, 16, 64$ without having to retune your learning rate $\eta$ or $\alpha$.

Which weight matrices inside a Transformer should actually get LoRA adapters? While the original 2021 paper only attached adapters to the attention query and value projections ($W_q, W_v$), Dettmers et al. (2023) showed that adapting every linear layer in the block—all four attention projections ($W_q, W_k, W_v, W_o$) plus all three feed-forward projections ($W_{\text{gate}}, W_{\text{up}}, W_{\text{down}}$)—at a modest rank like $r = 16$ consistently outperforms adapting only $W_q, W_v$ even at a huge rank like $r = 64$.

To squeeze that entire training run onto a single consumer GPU, QLoRA compresses the frozen base weights $W_0$ from 16-bit down to 4-bit NormalFloat4 (NF4) with double quantization ($\approx 0.55\text{ bytes/weight}$, shrinking a 7B model's base weights from $14\text{ GB}$ to $\sim 3.9\text{ GB}$). During each forward and backward step, QLoRA dequantizes blocks of $W_0$ on the fly into 16-bit BF16 registers to compute the matrix products ($W_0 x$ and $\bar{x} = W_0^\top \bar{h}$), while accumulating gradients exclusively into the 16-bit LoRA adapters $A$ and $B$.
