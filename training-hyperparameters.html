Training Hyperparameters: Why Runs Explode, Stall, or Overfit

TL;DR

  • The core coupling: Training hyperparameters are not independent dials. Learning rate ($\eta$) sets step length, batch size ($B$) sets gradient signal-to-noise, and gradient accumulation ($K$) lets small GPUs simulate large effective batches ($B_{\text{eff}} = B_{\text{micro}} \times K$).
  • Why runs explode at step 1: Above the stability ceiling $\eta > 2/\text{curvature}$, gradient descent bounces higher on every step. And in AdamW, the early variance estimate $v_t$ has tiny sample size on steps $1\text{--}100$, so you need linear warmup before decaying $\eta$ with a cosine schedule and gradient clipping.
  • Why AdamW replaced L2 regularization: Adding $\lambda w^2$ to the loss mixes weight decay into the adaptive denominator $\sqrt{v_t}$, shrinking heavily updated weights less than idle weights. AdamW decouples weight decay ($\lambda$) so every weight shrinks by the exact fraction $\eta \lambda$ per step.

1. Chapter 1: The Step-Size Cliff — Learning Rate ($\eta$) and Curvature

Every training story starts with the same disaster: you kick off a fine-tuning job at $\eta = 0.1$, walk away for coffee, and return five minutes later to find Loss: NaN.

Why doesn't a bigger learning rate simply reach the bottom of the valley faster? To see why there is a hard cliff between convergence and explosion, watch one weight $w$ move down a simple parabola $\mathcal{L}(w) = w^2$. Its minimum sits at $w^* = 0$, and its slope (gradient $g$) at any point is $g = 2w$.

Guess First: The Overshoot Cliff

Where Does $w_0 = 4$ Land When $\eta = 1.0$?

Suppose our weight starts at $w_0 = 4$ on the bowl $\mathcal{L}(w) = w^2$ (so the initial loss is $4^2 = 16$ and the slope is $g_0 = 2 \times 4 = 8$). Using the update rule $w_1 = w_0 - \eta g_0$ with $\eta = 1.0$, what happens to the loss after one step?

That symmetry reveals the most important number in optimization. On $\mathcal{L}(w) = w^2$, the second derivative (curvature $H$) is $2$. Whenever $\eta$ exceeds the stability limit $2 / H = 1.0$, each step jumps past zero and lands higher up the opposite wall than where it started.

$w_{t+1}$ $=$ $w_t$ $-$ $\eta$ $\cdot$ $g_t$
Gradient Descent Step (tap any symbol): New weight = Old weight minus (learning rate $\eta \times$ uphill slope $g_t$)

Worked Example (Tracing 3 steps across 3 learning rates from $w_0 = 4$):

Since $g_t = 2 w_t$, the update is $w_{t+1} = w_t - \eta(2 w_t) = w_t(1 - 2\eta)$. Compare three distinct settings of $\eta$:

  • Regime 1 — Too Small ($\eta = 0.25$): Multiplier is $1 - 2(0.25) = 0.5$. The weight halves each step: $w_0 = 4 \to w_1 = 2 \to w_2 = 1 \to w_3 = 0.5$. Smooth descent, no sign flip.
  • Regime 2 — Exact Optimum ($\eta = 0.5$): Multiplier is $1 - 2(0.5) = 0$. One step takes $w_0 = 4 \to w_1 = 4 - 0.5(8) = 0$.
  • Regime 3 — Past the Cliff ($\eta = 1.5$): Multiplier is $1 - 2(1.5) = -2$. The weight doubles and flips sign every step: $w_0 = 4 \to w_1 = -8 \to w_2 = +16 \to w_3 = -32$. By step 3, loss has exploded from $16$ to $1024$.

2. Chapter 2: Batch Size ($B$), Gradient Noise, and Accumulation ($K$)

In our parabola example, we knew the exact true slope $g_t$. In real machine learning, computing the true gradient requires running all $10\text{ million}$ training examples through the network before taking a single step.

Instead, Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) estimates the slope from a small mini-batch of $B$ examples. That introduces a crucial trade-off between speed, GPU memory, and gradient noise:

$\bar{g}_B$ $=$ $\dfrac{1}{B}$ $\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{B}$ $g^{(i)}$ $, \qquad$ $B_{\text{eff}}$ $=$ $B_{\text{micro}}$ $\times$ $K$
Tap any symbol: Mini-batch gradient averages $B$ sample gradients | Effective batch = micro-batch $\times$ accumulation steps $K$
Guess First: The Batch-Size Scaling Trap

You Quadruple Batch Size $B$ From $8$ to $32$ for the Same Number of Epochs

Suppose you train on $320$ examples for $1$ epoch. At $B = 8$, the optimizer takes $320 \div 8 = 40$ weight updates. If you switch to $B = 32$ without changing your learning rate $\eta$, what happens?

Worked Example (Tracing Gradient Accumulation across distinct sample gradients):

Suppose your GPU only has enough VRAM for a micro-batch of $B_{\text{micro}} = 2$ examples at a time, but you want the smooth, low-noise gradient of $B_{\text{eff}} = 4$. You set accumulation steps $K = 2$.

Four training examples have four distinct per-sample gradients: $g^{(1)} = 2$, $g^{(2)} = 10$, $g^{(3)} = 4$, and $g^{(4)} = 12$.

Micro-step 1 (Examples 1 & 2): Forward + backward pass on $[2, 10]$. Micro-batch average is $(2 + 10) \div 2 = 6$. Do not update weights yet! Store $6$ in the gradient buffer.
Micro-step 2 (Examples 3 & 4): Forward + backward pass on $[4, 12]$. Micro-batch average is $(4 + 12) \div 2 = 8$.
Optimizer Step (Average over $K = 2$ micro-steps): $\bar{g}_{\text{eff}}$ $= (6 + 8) \div 2 = 7$.
Notice that $(2 + 10 + 4 + 12) \div 4 = 28 \div 4 = 7$. You get the exact same gradient update as a single batch of $4$, using half the activation memory!

3. Chapter 3: Warmup, Cosine Decay, and Gradient Clipping ($c$)

Suppose you picked a sensible peak learning rate ($\eta_{\max} = 10^{-3}$) and a clean effective batch size ($B_{\text{eff}} = 64$). Why do modern Transformer runs still destabilize if you use a constant learning rate from step 1 to the end?

Because a training run passes through three completely different physical phases:

  1. Steps $1 \to W$ (Cold Start): At step 1, network weights are random and the AdamW optimizer has zero history of how noisy each weight's gradient is ($v_0 = 0$). Firing a full-size step immediately sends weights flying into saturated activations. Fix: Linear Warmup ramps $\eta_t$ smoothly from $0$ up to $\eta_{\max}$ over $W$ steps while optimizer statistics stabilize.
  2. Middle Steps (Fast Traversal): Near $\eta_{\max}$, large steps cross flat plateaus and bounce out of sharp, narrow traps.
  3. Final Steps (Settling into the Valley Floor): Because mini-batch gradients always contain random noise, a big learning rate keeps bouncing around the valley walls forever. Fix: Cosine Decay smoothly lowers $\eta_t$ down toward a floor $\eta_{\min}$ (typically $10\%$ of $\eta_{\max}$) so the weights settle to the bottom.
Warmup ($t \le W$):   $\eta_t$ $=$ $\eta_{\max}$ $\cdot \dfrac{t}{W}$   |   Clip:   $\tilde{g}$ $=$ $g$ $\cdot \min\!\left(1,\; \dfrac{c}{\lVert g \rVert_2}\right)$
Tap any symbol: $W$ = warmup steps | $c$ = max allowed gradient norm $\lVert g \rVert_2$

Worked Example (Mental-math trace of Warmup + Gradient Clipping):

Part A (Linear Warmup): Your peak learning rate is $\eta_{\max} = 10$ (in units of $10^{-4}$) with warmup length $W = 5$ steps.
At step $t = 1$: $\eta_1 = 10 \times (1/5) = 2$.
At step $t = 3$: $\eta_3 = 10 \times (3/5) = 6$.
At step $t = 5$: $\eta_5 = 10 \times (5/5) = 10$ (reaches full peak!).

Part B (Gradient Norm Clipping with distinct 3-4-5 triangle numbers): Suppose a corrupted batch causes a sudden gradient spike across two weights: $g = [6, 8]$, and your clip threshold is $c = 5$.
1. Compute the vector length (L2 norm $\lVert g \rVert_2$): $\sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{36 + 64} = \sqrt{100} = 10$.
2. Compare against threshold $c = 5$: Since $10 > 5$, scale the vector by $\dfrac{c}{\lVert g \rVert_2} = \dfrac{5}{10} = 0.5$.
3. Clipped gradient: $\tilde{g}$ $= 0.5 \times [6, 8] = [3, 4]$.
Check: The direction ($3:4$ ratio) is $100\%$ preserved, while the new length is $\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5 = c$.

Why Both Warmup AND Clipping?

Without looking above, state in one sentence what warmup protects against at the start of training versus what gradient clipping protects against mid-training.

Key distinction: Warmup prevents early instability during steps $1\text{--}W$ while random weights and AdamW's variance estimate $v_t$ are still uncalibrated; gradient clipping acts as a circuit breaker at any step $t$ by scaling down rare, extreme gradient spikes (where $\lVert g \rVert_2 > c$) without changing their direction.

4. Chapter 4: Inside AdamW — Momentum ($m_t$), Variance ($v_t$), and Decoupled Decay ($\lambda$)

Why did AdamW become the universal default optimizer for Transformers over plain SGD?

In a deep network, different layers have wildly different gradient scales: an embedding weight for a rare word might get a gradient of $0.001$, while an output projection weight gets a gradient of $10.0$. No single learning rate $\eta$ works for both under plain SGD.

AdamW solves this by keeping two running trackers for every individual weight:

  • 1. First Moment $m_t$ (Momentum, controlled by $\beta_1 \approx 0.9$): A smoothed moving average of the gradient $g_t$. If gradients keep pointing in the same direction, $m_t$ builds up speed; if gradients zig-zag positive and negative, they cancel out.
  • 2. Second Moment $v_t$ (Adaptive Scale, controlled by $\beta_2 \approx 0.999$): A moving average of the squared gradient $g_t^2$. Dividing the update by $\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \epsilon$ normalizes each weight by its typical gradient size—giving quiet weights bigger effective steps and loud weights smaller steps!
Guess First: Why AdamW Replaced L2 Regularization

Two Weights Both Have Value $w = 10$, But Different Gradient Histories

Suppose Weight A is noisy ($\sqrt{v_A} = 10$) and Weight B is quiet ($\sqrt{v_B} = 1$). We want to shrink both weights toward zero to prevent overfitting. What happens if we use classic L2 regularization (adding $\frac{\lambda}{2}w^2$ to the loss so $\lambda w$ gets added into the gradient before Adam divides by $\sqrt{v_t}$)?

That flaw is why Loshchilov & Hutter invented AdamW ("Adam with Decoupled Weight Decay"). Instead of mixing $\lambda w_t$ into the gradient $g_t$, AdamW applies weight decay directly to the weight outside the adaptive $\hat{m}_t / \sqrt{\hat{v}_t}$ term:

$w_{t+1}$ $=$ $w_t$ $-$ $\underbrace{\eta \cdot \lambda \cdot w_t}_{\text{1. Decoupled Weight Decay}}$ $-$ $\underbrace{\eta \cdot \dfrac{\hat{m}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \epsilon}}_{\text{2. Adaptive Gradient Step}}$
Tap any symbol: $\hat{m}_t = \frac{m_t}{1 - \beta_1^t}$ (bias-corrected momentum) | $\hat{v}_t = \frac{v_t}{1 - \beta_2^t}$ (bias-corrected variance) | $\epsilon \approx 10^{-8}$

Worked Example (Tracing One Complete AdamW Step by Hand—Mental Math Only):

Let's trace a single weight starting at $w_1 = 10$ using distinct, clean numbers: $\eta = 0.5$, $\lambda = 0.2$, and after bias correction our smoothed momentum is $\hat{m}_1 = 6$ while our smoothed squared gradient is $\hat{v}_1 = 9$ (with tiny $\epsilon = 0$ for mental arithmetic).

Part 1 — Decoupled Weight Decay ($\eta \cdot \lambda \cdot w_1$):
Multiply $\eta \times \lambda = 0.5 \times 0.2 = 0.1$ ($10\%$ shrinkage).
Weight decay subtracts $0.1 \times 10 = 1$, pulling $w_1$ from $10$ down to $9$.

Part 2 — Adaptive Gradient Step ($\eta \cdot \frac{\hat{m}_1}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_1}}$):
1. Take the square root of variance: $\sqrt{\hat{v}_1} = \sqrt{9} = 3$.
2. Normalize momentum by scale: $\dfrac{\hat{m}_1}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_1}} = \dfrac{6}{3} = 2$.
3. Multiply by learning rate $\eta = 0.5$: $0.5 \times 2 = 1$.

Part 3 — Final Updated Weight ($w_2$):
$w_2$ $= 10 - \underbrace{1}_{\text{decay}} - \underbrace{1}_{\text{adaptive step}} = 8$.

Why do $\hat{m}_t$ and $\hat{v}_t$ have hats? (Bias Correction Derivation)

At step $t = 0$, Adam initializes both trackers to zero: $m_0 = 0$ and $v_0 = 0$. On step $t = 1$ with $\beta_1 = 0.9$: $$m_1 = 0.9(0) + 0.1 g_1 = 0.1 g_1$$ That means $m_1$ is biased toward zero—it is only $10\%$ of the true gradient $g_1$! Dividing by $(1 - \beta_1^1) = (1 - 0.9) = 0.1$ restores the true scale: $$\hat{m}_1 = \frac{0.1 g_1}{1 - 0.9} = g_1$$ As $t$ grows large, $\beta_1^t \to 0$, so the bias correction denominator $(1 - \beta_1^t) \to 1$ and automatically disappears.
