Hierarchical Multiclass Classification: Taxonomy Losses, Chain-Rule Routing & Rollup

Most real-world classification catalogs are not flat lists of unrelated labels; they are trees. An e-commerce catalog splits into Apparel and Electronics, Apparel splits into Footwear and Outerwear, and Footwear splits into fine-grained leaves like Boot, Sneaker, and RunningSandal. Similarly, medical billing codes (ICD-10) and biological species sit inside strict parent-child taxonomies where every leaf belongs to a hierarchy of broader categories.

If you ignore that tree and train a standard flat softmax classifier over all $L$ leaf classes with cross-entropy, two bad things happen. During training, flat cross-entropy treats mistaking a Pug for its sibling Husky as the exact same penalty as mistaking a Pug for a DumpTruck. Worse, at test time, picking the single leaf with the highest score (flat leaf argmax) falls victim to a vote-splitting trap: whenever a model is sure an item belongs to a broad branch like Footwear ($60\%$ total probability) but unsure which exact shoe style it is, it splits that $60\%$ across several sibling shoe leaves—letting an unrelated, unsplit leaf like Cable ($40\%$) steal first place.

Respecting the tree fixes both problems. By coupling parent and child probabilities—either bottom-up by summing leaf probabilities into their parent branches (rollup), or top-down by multiplying local parent-to-child conditionals (chain-rule factorization)—the model keeps its probability mass consistent across every level of the tree and can safely back off to a parent category like Footwear whenever the leaf-level photo is too blurry to call.

2. Coupling Parent and Leaf Probabilities: Chain Rule vs. Bottom-Up Rollup

In a strict tree taxonomy, every leaf $y$ has a single unique path of ancestor nodes $\text{Anc}(y)$ leading down from the root, and the leaves under any parent node $u$ are mutually exclusive events. Basic probability demands one consistency law across the tree: a parent node's probability must equal the exact sum of its children's probabilities.

If you violate that law by training independent binary sigmoid classifiers at every node, your model will regularly output embarrassing hierarchy violations at test time—like predicting $P(\text{Pug}) = 0.80$ while its own parent node predicts $P(\text{Dog}) = 0.20$. Instead, you can guarantee $100\%$ tree consistency in one of two mathematically equivalent ways.

The first way is top-down chain-rule factorization: place a small local softmax at each parent node $\text{pa}(v)$ that only predicts conditional probabilities $P(v \mid \text{pa}(v))$ among its immediate children. To get the joint probability of a leaf at the bottom of the tree, you multiply the conditional probabilities along its ancestor path $\text{Anc}(y)$. And because the logarithm of a product is the sum of the logarithms, the hierarchical cross-entropy loss of a leaf simply adds the local cross-entropy losses at every level along its path.

The second way is bottom-up marginal rollup: keep a single flat softmax head over all $L$ leaves, and compute any internal parent node's probability $P(u)$ by summing the probabilities of all descendant leaves in its subtree: $P(u) = \sum_{\ell \in \text{Leaves}(u)} P(\ell)$. Even with a single leaf softmax head, you can add a multi-level training loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = -\log P(y) - \lambda \log P(\text{pa}(y))$ so that rare leaves with only a handful of training examples still get pulled into the right parent branch by thousands of sibling examples.

3. Grading Mistakes by Tree Distance & Backing Off to a Parent Node

Even a well-trained classifier will sometimes face an ambiguous input where it cannot tell two sibling leaves apart. When that happens, flat accuracy treats every non-exact prediction as a total $0\%$ failure. In a hierarchy, we care about how far the prediction landed from the truth.

We can measure that severity in two ways. First, tree distance $d_T(y, \hat{y})$ counts the shortest-path edges between the true node $y$ and predicted node $\hat{y}$ through their Lowest Common Ancestor (LCA): two siblings like Pug and Husky sit just $2$ edges apart (up to Dog and back down), whereas cousins in different subtrees sit $4$ or $6$ edges apart. Second, Hierarchical Precision and Recall ($hP, hR, hF_1$) compare the full set of non-root ancestors $\text{Anc}(\hat{y})$ against the true ancestor path $\text{Anc}(y)$, giving partial credit for every shared ancestor node along the branch:

$$hP = \frac{|\text{Anc}(y) \cap \text{Anc}(\hat{y})|}{|\text{Anc}(\hat{y})|}, \qquad hR = \frac{|\text{Anc}(y) \cap \text{Anc}(\hat{y})|}{|\text{Anc}(y)|}, \qquad \hat{y}_{\text{CRM}} = \arg\min_{\hat{y}} \sum_{y} P(y)\, C(y, \hat{y})$$
Look at the denominator of $hP$: $|\text{Anc}(\hat{y})|$ is the depth of your prediction. If no leaf is confident enough, selective backoff stops at the internal parent node (say, Dog at depth 2 instead of guessing a wrong breed at depth 3), shrinking the denominator of $hP$ so every predicted ancestor is $100\%$ accurate!

Tracing Hierarchical Precision, Recall & Backoff on $\text{Animal} \to \text{Dog} \to \text{Pug}$:

True label $y = \text{Pug}$ has 3 non-root ancestors: $\text{Anc}(y) = \{\text{Animal},\; \text{Dog},\; \text{Pug}\}$ ($|\text{Anc}(y)| = 3$). Compare three predictions:
• Wrong top-level branch ($\hat{y} = \text{Truck}$): $\text{Anc}(\hat{y}) = \{\text{Vehicle},\; \text{Auto},\; \text{Truck}\}$. Zero overlap $\implies hP = \mathbf{0\%}$, $hR = \mathbf{0\%}$.
• Wrong sibling leaf ($\hat{y} = \text{Husky}$): $\text{Anc}(\hat{y}) = \{\text{Animal},\; \text{Dog},\; \text{Husky}\}$ ($3$ nodes). Shared ancestors $= \{\text{Animal},\; \text{Dog}\}$ ($2$ nodes) $\implies hP = \mathbf{\frac{2}{3}}$, $hR = \mathbf{\frac{2}{3}}$.
• Selective backoff to parent ($\hat{y} = \text{Dog}$): Instead of guessing between Pug and Husky, the model outputs internal node Dog, so $\text{Anc}(\hat{y}) = \{\text{Animal},\; \text{Dog}\}$ ($|\text{Anc}(\hat{y})| = 2$). Both predicted nodes are correct ($|\text{Anc}(y) \cap \text{Anc}(\hat{y})| = 2$), keeping recall at $hR = \frac{2}{3}$.

4. Hands-On Practice: Minimizing Expected Tree Distance

Instead of picking whichever leaf has the highest raw probability, Conditional Risk Minimization (CRM) picks the node $\hat{y}$ that minimizes expected tree distance $\mathbb{E}[d_T(y, \hat{y})]$. Notice how weighting errors by tree distance automatically rescues a split branch without even needing a hand-tuned threshold.

Try It By Hand

Pick the Leaf That Minimizes Expected Tree Distance

In our 3-leaf tree, sibling leaves $A_1$ and $A_2$ sit $d_T(A_1, A_2) = 2$ edges apart, while cousin leaf $B_1$ sits $d_T = 4$ edges away from both $A_1$ and $A_2$. The model outputs:
$$P(A_1) = 0.30, \qquad P(A_2) = 0.30, \qquad P(B_1) = 0.40$$ Step 1 (Expected distance if you pick flat winner $\hat{y} = B_1$):
If $A_1$ is true ($0.30$), you miss by $4$ edges; if $A_2$ is true ($0.30$), you miss by $4$ edges; if $B_1$ is true ($0.40$), distance is $0$:
$$\mathbb{E}[d_T \mid \hat{y} = B_1] = 0.30(4) + 0.30(4) + 0.40(0) = 1.2 + 1.2 + 0 = \mathbf{2.4\text{ edges}}$$ Step 2 (Your turn — Expected distance if you pick $\hat{y} = A_1$):
