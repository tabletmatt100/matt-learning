Hierarchical Multiclass Classification: Taxonomy Losses, Chain-Rule Routing & Rollup

Most real-world classification catalogs are not flat lists of unrelated labels; they are trees. An e-commerce catalog splits into Apparel and Electronics, Apparel splits into Footwear and Outerwear, and Footwear splits into fine-grained leaves like Boot, Sneaker, and RunningSandal. Similarly, medical billing codes (ICD-10) and biological species sit inside strict parent-child taxonomies where every leaf belongs to a hierarchy of broader categories.

If you ignore that tree and train a standard flat softmax classifier over all $L$ leaf classes with cross-entropy, two bad things happen. During training, flat cross-entropy treats mistaking a Pug for its sibling Husky as the exact same penalty as mistaking a Pug for a DumpTruck. Worse, at test time, picking the single leaf with the highest score (flat leaf argmax) falls victim to a vote-splitting trap: whenever a model is sure an item belongs to a broad branch like Footwear ($60\%$ total probability) but unsure which exact shoe style it is, it splits that $60\%$ across several sibling shoe leaves—letting an unrelated, unsplit leaf like Cable ($40\%$) steal first place.

Respecting the tree fixes both problems. By coupling parent and child probabilities—either bottom-up by summing leaf probabilities into their parent branches (rollup), or top-down by multiplying local parent-to-child conditionals (chain-rule factorization)—the model keeps its probability mass consistent across every level of the tree and can safely back off to a parent category like Footwear whenever a photo is too blurry to call at the leaf level.

1. The Split-Vote Trap & Coupling Parent-Leaf Probabilities

To see the vote-splitting trap in action, take a 3-leaf product catalog where Apparel splits into two sibling footwear leaves (Boot and Sneaker) while Electronics has a single unsplit leaf (Cable). A seller uploads a blurry photo of a dark shoe. Because the classifier cannot tell whether the shoe is a boot or a sneaker, it divides the footwear mass evenly between the two siblings:

$$P(\text{Boot}) = 30\%, \qquad P(\text{Sneaker}) = 30\%, \qquad P(\text{Cable}) = 40\%$$

If you use naive flat leaf argmax, the classifier compares $40\%$ against $30\%$ and predicts Cable—even though Apparel holds $30\% + 30\% = 60\%$ of the probability mass!

In a strict tree taxonomy, every leaf $y$ has a single unique path of ancestor nodes $\text{Anc}(y)$ leading down from the root, and the leaves under any parent node $u$ are mutually exclusive events. Basic probability demands one consistency law across the tree: a parent node's probability must equal the exact sum of its children's probabilities. If you violate that law by training independent binary sigmoid heads at every node, your model can output embarrassing hierarchy violations like $P(\text{Pug}) = 0.80$ while its own parent predicts $P(\text{Dog}) = 0.20$.

Instead, you can guarantee $100\%$ tree consistency in one of two equivalent ways. With bottom-up marginal rollup, you keep a single flat softmax head over all $L$ leaves, and compute any internal parent node's probability $P(u)$ by summing its descendant leaves: $P(u) = \sum_{\ell \in \text{Leaves}(u)} P(\ell)$. With top-down chain-rule factorization, you place a small local softmax at each parent node $\text{pa}(v)$ over its immediate children, so a leaf's probability is the product of conditionals along its path (and its cross-entropy loss is the sum of local path losses):

$$P(y) = \prod_{v \in \text{Anc}(y)} P\bigl(v \mid \text{pa}(v)\bigr), \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{hier}}(y) = -\log_2 P(y) = \sum_{v \in \text{Anc}(y)} \bigl[-\log_2 P\bigl(v \mid \text{pa}(v)\bigr)\bigr]$$

Worked Example (Tracing top-down chain-rule loss in base-2 bits on $\text{Root} \to \text{Animal} \to \text{Dog} \to \text{Pug}$):

Suppose at each split along the path to Pug, our local classifiers assign distinct powers of $\frac{1}{2}$:
1. Level 1 (Root $\to$ Animal vs. Vehicle): $P(\text{Animal} \mid \text{Root}) = \mathbf{\frac{1}{2}} \implies -\log_2 P = \mathbf{1\text{ bit}}$.
2. Level 2 (Animal $\to$ Dog vs. Cat): $P(\text{Dog} \mid \text{Animal}) = \mathbf{\frac{1}{4}} \implies -\log_2 P = \mathbf{2\text{ bits}}$.
3. Level 3 (Dog $\to$ Pug vs. Husky): $P(\text{Pug} \mid \text{Dog}) = \mathbf{\frac{1}{8}} \implies -\log_2 P = \mathbf{3\text{ bits}}$.
Multiplying the three conditionals gives joint leaf probability $P(\text{Pug}) = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{4} \times \frac{1}{8} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{64}}$, and adding the three bit losses gives total hierarchical cross-entropy $\mathcal{L}_{\text{hier}}(\text{Pug}) = 1 + 2 + 3 = \mathbf{6\text{ bits}}$.

2. Tree Distance, Ancestor F1 ($hF_1$) & Selective Backoff

Even a well-trained classifier will sometimes face an ambiguous input where it cannot tell two sibling leaves apart. When that happens, flat accuracy treats every non-exact prediction as a total $0\%$ failure. In a hierarchy, we care about how far the prediction landed from the truth.

We can measure that severity in two ways. First, tree distance $d_T(y, \hat{y})$ counts the shortest-path edges between the true node $y$ and predicted node $\hat{y}$ through their Lowest Common Ancestor (LCA): two siblings like Pug and Husky sit just $2$ edges apart (up to Dog and back down), whereas cousins in different subtrees sit $4$ or $6$ edges apart. Second, Hierarchical Precision and Recall ($hP, hR, hF_1$) compare the predicted non-root ancestor set $\text{Anc}(\hat{y})$ against the true ancestor path $\text{Anc}(y)$, giving partial credit for every shared branch node:

$$hP = \frac{|\text{Anc}(y) \cap \text{Anc}(\hat{y})|}{|\text{Anc}(\hat{y})|}, \qquad hR = \frac{|\text{Anc}(y) \cap \text{Anc}(\hat{y})|}{|\text{Anc}(y)|}, \qquad \hat{y}_{\text{CRM}} = \arg\min_{\hat{y}} \sum_{y} P(y)\, C(y, \hat{y})$$

Look closely at the denominator of $hP$: $|\text{Anc}(\hat{y})|$ is the depth of your prediction. Suppose the true label is Pug, which has $3$ non-root ancestors $\text{Anc}(y) = \{\text{Animal},\; \text{Dog},\; \text{Pug}\}$. If your model guesses the wrong sibling Husky ($\text{Anc} = \{\text{Animal},\; \text{Dog},\; \text{Husky}\}$), it gets $2$ out of $3$ ancestors right ($hP = \frac{2}{3} = 66.7\%$, $hR = \frac{2}{3} = 66.7\%$).

What if the model instead uses selective backoff and stops at the internal parent node $\hat{y} = \text{Dog}$ ($\text{Anc}(\hat{y}) = \{\text{Animal},\; \text{Dog}\}$)? It still captures the exact same $2$ true ancestors ($hR = \frac{2}{3} = 66.7\%$), but because it only predicted $2$ nodes and both are right, its hierarchical precision jumps to $hP = \frac{2}{2} = \mathbf{100\%}$! Similarly, Conditional Risk Minimization (CRM) picks the node that minimizes expected tree-distance cost $\mathbb{E}[d_T(y, \hat{y})]$, automatically favoring a split branch over an isolated wrong branch.

The true label is leaf Pug with 3 non-root ancestors $\text{Anc}(y) = \{\text{Animal}, \text{Dog}, \text{Pug}\}$. Why does backing off to internal node $\hat{y} = \text{Dog}$ ($\text{Anc}(\hat{y}) = \{\text{Animal}, \text{Dog}\}$) raise hierarchical precision ($hP$) compared to guessing wrong sibling Husky, while keeping hierarchical recall ($hR$) identical?

In our 3-leaf tree, sibling leaves $A_1, A_2$ sit $d_T(A_1, A_2) = 2$ edges apart, while cousin leaf $B_1$ sits $d_T = 4$ edges away from both $A_1$ and $A_2$. For a test item with $P(A_1) = 0.30$, $P(A_2) = 0.30$, and $P(B_1) = 0.40$, predicting flat winner $B_1$ has expected tree distance $0.30(4) + 0.30(4) + 0.40(0) = \mathbf{2.4\text{ edges}}$.
