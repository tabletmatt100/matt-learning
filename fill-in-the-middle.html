Fill-in-the-Middle (FIM): Teaching Causal LLMs to Read the Future Without Changing a Single Weight

Think about how you actually write code in an editor. Almost never do you start on line 1 and type straight through to the bottom of the file without looking back. Usually your cursor sits in the middle of an existing function: you have imports and a function header above your cursor (the prefix $P$), a return statement and closing braces below your cursor (the suffix $S$), and you want an AI assistant to fill in the missing logic right where your cursor is (the middle $M$).

A standard left-to-right autoregressive LLM has a structural blind spot here. Because its causal attention mask blocks every token from looking to the right, a normal GPT model generating at your cursor can only see the prefix $P$ above it. It has no idea that three lines below your cursor, the function returns total_tax—so it might invent a completely different variable name or re-type the return statement a second time.

In 2022, Mohammad Bavarian and colleagues at OpenAI showed that you don't need a bidirectional encoder-decoder to fix this. Their technique, Fill-in-the-Middle (FIM), is a pure data transformation: during training, you take a fraction of your documents, slice each one into three pieces $(P, M, S)$, and move the middle span $M$ to the very end of the sequence using three special sentinel tokens. Because $P$ and $S$ now sit to the left of $M$, a completely standard causal Transformer can attend to both the prefix and the suffix while generating the middle token by token.

1. The Permute-and-Sentinel Trick: PSM vs. SPM

How do you rearrange a document $D = (P, M, S)$ so a left-to-right model knows where the cut happened and where the suffix begins? Before tokenizing—or at the token level after choosing random cut points—you split the document into three contiguous spans and glue them back together with three dedicated sentinel tokens: <PRE> (prefix starts), <SUF> (suffix starts), and <MID> (middle starts).

There are two ways to order the prefix and suffix before generating the middle. In PSM (Prefix–Suffix–Middle) mode, the prefix comes first, followed by the suffix, and then the middle. In SPM (Suffix–Prefix–Middle) mode, you put the suffix out front so that the prefix $P$ flows directly into the middle $M$:

$$\text{PSM:}\quad \texttt{\textless PRE\textgreater}\;\circ\; P \;\circ\; \texttt{\textless SUF\textgreater}\;\circ\; S \;\circ\; \texttt{\textless MID\textgreater}\;\circ\; M \;\circ\; \texttt{\textless EOT\textgreater}$$ $$\text{SPM:}\quad \texttt{\textless PRE\textgreater}\;\circ\; \texttt{\textless SUF\textgreater}\;\circ\; S \;\circ\; \texttt{\textless MID\textgreater}\;\circ\; P \;\circ\; M \;\circ\; \texttt{\textless EOT\textgreater}$$
Figure 1: Moving the middle span $M$ to the end lets a causal decoder condition on both $P$ and $S$. In PSM, a sentinel <MID> separates the prompt from $M$. In SPM, the prefix $P$ sits right after <MID> so $P$ and $M$ form one unbroken span—making IDE KV-cache reuse seamless as the programmer keeps typing.

Wait—look closely at the SPM formula above. Why does <MID> come before the prefix $P$ instead of between $P$ and $M$? If you didn't put a sentinel between $P$ and $M$, how would the model know where the prefix ended? During training, it doesn't need to! From the model's perspective after <MID>, it is simply generating the combined text $P \circ M$ conditioned on the suffix $S$. And at inference time in an IDE, this gives SPM two huge practical advantages over PSM: first, there is no artificial sentinel token interrupting the natural token flow between the end of the user's cursor prefix $P$ and the first generated token of $M$; second, as the user types another character onto $P$ while the suffix $S$ stays fixed, the server can keep its KV-cache for <PRE> <SUF> S <MID> P and just append the newly typed token!

In practice, modern code models like CodeLlama and StarCoder train on a $50/50$ mix of PSM and SPM so the model supports both formats at inference time. And notice how the model knows when to stop filling in the middle: during training, an end-of-text sentinel <EOT> is placed right after $M$. When the model generates <EOT> at inference time, the IDE stops sampling immediately—signaling that the generated middle has cleanly connected into the first line of the suffix $S$.

2. The "FIM-for-Free" Property & Character-Level Splitting

Here is the question that worried everyone before the OpenAI FIM paper came out: if you turn $50\%$ or $90\%$ of your pre-training documents into scrambled $(P, S, M)$ puzzles, doesn't that hurt the model's normal left-to-right autoregressive generation quality?

Surprisingly, no. Bavarian et al. proved what they call the FIM-for-free property: training a causal language model from scratch with a FIM transformation rate $p_{\text{FIM}} = 0.5$ (and even up to $0.9$) yields the exact same left-to-right test loss and HumanEval pass@1 score as a $100\%$ left-to-right model trained for the same number of tokens, while adding full infilling capability at zero extra FLOP cost.

Why doesn't FIM waste training capacity? Because unlike BERT's masked language modeling—which computes loss only on the $15\%$ masked tokens—FIM still computes standard next-token cross-entropy loss across all three spans ($P$, $S$, and $M$) in every training step! On a document with $|P| + |M| + |S| = N$ content tokens plus $4$ sentinel tokens, the total sequence length is $N + 4$:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{FIM}} = -\sum_{t \in P \cup S \cup M \cup \text{sentinels}} \log P_\theta\!\left(x_t \;\middle|\; x_{<t}\right)$$

While the model scans across $P$, it is doing pure left-to-right language modeling. While it scans across $S$, it is still doing left-to-right language modeling (with a sudden jump at the cut). And while it scans across $M$, it gets the harder and more informative task of bridging $P$ into $S$.

Worked Example (Tracing token positions & causal context on a 9-token document):

Suppose a document has $N = 9$ content tokens split into $|P| = 4$ tokens, $|M| = 2$ tokens, and $|S| = 3$ tokens.
1. PSM sequence length: Wrapping $(P, S, M)$ with the $4$ sentinels (<PRE>, <SUF>, <MID>, <EOT>) produces a sequence of length $4 + 3 + 2 + 4 = \mathbf{13\text{ tokens}}$.
2. Context seen when predicting the first token of $M$: Right after <MID>, how many preceding tokens sit in the causal window? The sentinel <PRE> ($1$), the prefix $P$ ($4$), the sentinel <SUF> ($1$), the suffix $S$ ($3$), and <MID> ($1$) $\implies 1 + 4 + 1 + 3 + 1 = \mathbf{10\text{ tokens}}$ (all $4$ prefix tokens and all $3$ suffix tokens).
3. Token overhead: Adding $4$ sentinels to a $2{,}048$-token training chunk adds only $4 / 2048 \approx \mathbf{0.2\%}$ extra tokens.

There is one subtle trap that breaks FIM in real editors if you get it wrong during data preparation: always split documents in raw character space before running BPE tokenization, never in token space (or decode and re-encode the three spans after splitting). Why? Imagine the user's cursor sits in the middle of the identifier calculate_tax: the prefix ends with "def calc" and the suffix starts with "ax(price):", so the missing middle is "ulate_t". In the original unsplit file, the BPE tokenizer merged "calculate" into a single token ID. If you only ever split training examples along existing BPE token boundaries, the model never sees sub-word fragments at the edges of $P$, $M$, and $S$—and fails miserably whenever a programmer's cursor sits mid-word! Splitting at random character indices and tokenizing $P$, $M$, and $S$ separately teaches the model to stitch sub-word BPE fragments together cleanly.

A training chunk of $N = 20$ content tokens is split into a prefix of $|P| = 8$ tokens, a middle of $|M| = 5$ tokens, and a suffix of $|S| = 7$ tokens, then formatted in PSM mode: <PRE> P <SUF> S <MID> M <EOT>.
