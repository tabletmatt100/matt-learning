Linear Algebra for ML Refresher: Only the Parts You Actually Use

University linear algebra courses spend months on cofactor determinants, Cramer's rule, and hand-reducing $4 \times 4$ matrices to row-echelon form. In day-to-day machine learning and deep learning, you use almost none of that.

Instead, 95% of ML linear algebra comes down to three working skills: using dot products, $L_1/L_2$ norms, and cosine similarity to measure vector size and alignment; tracking matrix shapes $(B \times d_{\text{in}})\cdot(d_{\text{in}} \times d_{\text{out}})$ and transposes through forward and backward passes; and understanding how matrix rank, eigenvectors, and SVD compress high-dimensional matrices in PCA and LoRA.

1. Vectors, Norms ($L_1$ vs. $L_2$), Dot Products & Cosine Similarity

In ML, a vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a point or arrow in $d$-dimensional space representing an example's features or a token's embedding. To measure a single vector's size, we use the $L_2$ Euclidean length $\|x\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_i x_i^2}$ or the $L_1$ Manhattan sum $\|x\|_1 = \sum_i |x_i|$. To measure how strongly two vectors $a$ and $b$ align, we take their dot product—or divide out their lengths to get pure directional cosine similarity:

$$a \cdot b = a^\top b = \sum_{i=1}^{d} a_i b_i = \|a\|_2\,\|b\|_2\cos\theta, \qquad \cos\theta = \frac{a \cdot b}{\|a\|_2\,\|b\|_2} \in [-1, 1]$$

Worked Example (Dot product vs. Cosine similarity in 2D with clean 3-4-5 right triangles):

Take query vector $a = [3,\; 4]$ and compare it against $b = [6,\; 8]$ and $c = [4,\; -3]$:
1. Norms: $\|a\|_2 = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{25} = \mathbf{5}$ (and $\|a\|_1 = |3| + |4| = \mathbf{7}$). Meanwhile $\|b\|_2 = \sqrt{36 + 64} = \mathbf{10}$, and $\|c\|_2 = \sqrt{16 + 9} = \mathbf{5}$.
2. Same direction ($a$ and $b = 2a$): $a \cdot b = 3(6) + 4(8) = 18 + 32 = \mathbf{50}$, and $\cos\theta = \frac{50}{5 \times 10} = \mathbf{+1.00}$ (doubling a vector's length doubles its dot product, but leaves cosine similarity at $+1.00$).
3. Perpendicular / Orthogonal ($a$ and $c$): $a \cdot c = 3(4) + 4(-3) = 12 - 12 = \mathbf{0} \implies \cos\theta = \mathbf{0.00}$ ($90^\circ$ angle, zero shared signal).

Let $a = [3,\; 4]$ and $b = [0,\; 2]$. Without a calculator, what are $a \cdot b$, $\|a\|_2$, $\|b\|_2$, and $\cos\theta$?

2. Matrix Multiplication Shapes, Transposes & Low-Rank Compression (SVD / PCA / LoRA)

Every dense layer in a neural network computes a matrix multiplication $Y = XW$, where input batch $X$ has shape $(B \times d_{\text{in}})$ and weight matrix $W$ has shape $(d_{\text{in}} \times d_{\text{out}})$. An $(m \times \mathbf{k})$ matrix can only multiply a $(\mathbf{k} \times n)$ matrix when their inner dimension $\mathbf{k}$ matches; the inner $\mathbf{k}$ cancels out and leaves outer shape $(m \times n)$.

That single shape-cancellation rule gives you the matrix backpropagation formulas for free! Given upstream gradient $dY$ of shape $(B \times d_{\text{out}})$, there is only one way to arrange $W$ or $X$ using a transpose ($^\top$) so the inner dimensions match:

$$\underbrace{Y}_{(B \times d_{\text{out}})} = \underbrace{X}_{(B \times d_{\text{in}})} \underbrace{W}_{(d_{\text{in}} \times d_{\text{out}})} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \underbrace{dX}_{(B \times d_{\text{in}})} = \underbrace{dY}_{(B \times d_{\text{out}})} \underbrace{W^\top}_{(d_{\text{out}} \times d_{\text{in}})}, \qquad \underbrace{dW}_{(d_{\text{in}} \times d_{\text{out}})} = \underbrace{X^\top}_{(d_{\text{in}} \times B)} \underbrace{dY}_{(B \times d_{\text{out}})}$$

What does a matrix actually do geometrically? Multiplying a vector $v$ by a square matrix $A$ usually both rotates and stretches $v$. The special directions that $A$ stretches without rotating ($Av = \lambda v$) are its eigenvectors, and the stretch factor $\lambda$ is the eigenvalue. For any rectangular matrix $M \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) generalizes this by factoring $M = U \Sigma V^\top = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top$ into a sum of $r$ rank-1 outer products sorted by strength $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0$.

The number of non-zero singular values $r$ is the matrix's rank (the true number of independent directions it passes through). Keeping only the top $k \ll r$ singular values gives the optimal low-rank compression of a dataset (PCA), and factoring a $d \times d$ weight update directly into two rank-$r$ matrices $\Delta W = B A$ of shapes $(d \times r)$ and $(r \times d)$ is the entire mechanism behind LoRA!

In a linear layer $Y = XW$, one mini-batch has $B = 4$ tokens, input dimension $d_{\text{in}} = 3$, and output dimension $d_{\text{out}} = 5$ (so $X$ is $4 \times 3$ and $W$ is $3 \times 5$). Given upstream gradient $dY$ of shape $(4 \times 5)$, which expression computes the weight gradient $dW$ of shape $(3 \times 5)$?

Suppose diagonal matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ multiplies vector $v = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. What is $Av$, what is the eigenvalue $\lambda$ for eigenvector $v$, and what is the rank of $A$?
