Linear Algebra for ML Refresher: Only the Parts You Actually Use

TL;DR

  • Skip the textbook busywork: In machine learning, you will never compute a $4\times 4$ determinant by hand, invert a matrix with cofactors, use Cramer's rule, or row-reduce matrices to echelon form on paper.
  • Dot products & norms measure similarity and size: The dot product $a \cdot b = \sum_i a_i b_i = \|a\|_2 \|b\|_2 \cos\theta$ drives every neural layer and attention score. Dividing out the $L_2$ lengths gives cosine similarity ($\cos\theta$), which compares direction alone.
  • Shapes tell you the backprop formula for free: If a linear layer computes $Y = XW$ with $X \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times d_{\text{in}}}$ and $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}} \times d_{\text{out}}}$, the inner dimensions ($d_{\text{in}}$) must match and cancel. During backprop, transposes ($dX = dY\,W^\top$ and $dW = X^\top dY$) are uniquely forced by shape matching.
  • Rank & SVD explain compression (PCA and LoRA): A matrix's rank is the number of independent directions it passes through. Factoring a huge weight update $\Delta W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ into two skinny matrices $B A$ of inner width $r \ll d$ (LoRA) cuts parameters from $d^2$ to $2dr$.

1. Vectors, Norms, Dot Products, and Cosine Similarity

In ML, a vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is just a list of $d$ numbers representing features or an embedding. Almost everything we do with two vectors boils down to two questions: how big is each vector? (norm) and do they point in the same direction? (dot product / cosine similarity).

$$a \cdot b = a^\top b = \sum_{i=1}^{d} a_i b_i = \|a\|_2 \, \|b\|_2 \cos\theta, \qquad \text{Cosine Similarity} = \cos\theta = \frac{a \cdot b}{\|a\|_2 \, \|b\|_2}$$
Tap terms: $a \cdot b$ (dot product) · $\|a\|_2$ ($L_2$ Euclidean length) · $\cos\theta$ (direction alignment in $[-1, 1]$)

Worked mental-math example ($d = 2$):

Suppose query vector $a = [3, 4]$ and two candidate document vectors are $b = [6, 8]$ and $c = [4, -3]$.

1. $L_2$ Norm (Euclidean length $\sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2}$): $\|a\|_2 = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \mathbf{5}$. Meanwhile $\|b\|_2 = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = \mathbf{10}$, and $\|c\|_2 = \sqrt{4^2 + (-3)^2} = \mathbf{5}$.
2. $L_1$ Norm (Manhattan sum $|x_1| + |x_2|$): $\|a\|_1 = |3| + |4| = \mathbf{7}$.
3. Dot product & Cosine similarity:
• $a \cdot b = (3)(6) + (4)(8) = 18 + 32 = \mathbf{50}$, and $\cos\theta = \frac{50}{5 \times 10} = \mathbf{+1.00}$ (exact same direction; $b$ is just $2\times$ longer).
• $a \cdot c = (3)(4) + (4)(-3) = 12 - 12 = \mathbf{0}$, and $\cos\theta = \frac{0}{5 \times 5} = \mathbf{0.00}$ (orthogonal / $90^\circ$ perpendicular: zero shared signal).

Why $L_1$ regularization zeroes weights out while $L_2$ shrinks them smoothly

For a small weight $w = 0.01$, its $L_2$ penalty ($w^2 = 0.0001$) has derivative $2w = 0.02$, which gets weaker and weaker near zero—so $L_2$ weight decay shrinks weights toward zero without hitting exact zero. The $L_1$ penalty ($|w| = 0.01$) has derivative $\text{sign}(w) = \pm 1$ all the way down to $0$, pushing noisy features all the way to exact zero (sparsity).

2. Matrix Multiplication & Shape Tracking (The #1 Daily ML Skill)

Every dense layer (nn.Linear(d_in, d_out)) and every attention projection is a matrix multiplication $Y = XW$. You only need two mental pictures of $Y = XW$:

  1. Cell-by-cell (dot product view): Entry $Y_{i,j}$ is the dot product of row $i$ of $X$ (example $i$'s features) with column $j$ of $W$ (neuron $j$'s weights).
  2. Shape cancellation rule: An $(m \times \mathbf{k})$ matrix can only multiply a $(\mathbf{k} \times n)$ matrix because the inner dimension $\mathbf{k}$ matches. The inner $\mathbf{k}$ cancels out, leaving outer shape $(m \times n)$.
$$\underbrace{Y}_{(B \times d_{\text{out}})} = \underbrace{X}_{(B \times d_{\text{in}})} \underbrace{W}_{(d_{\text{in}} \times d_{\text{out}})} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial X}}_{(B \times d_{\text{in}})} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial Y}}_{(B \times d_{\text{out}})} \underbrace{W^\top}_{(d_{\text{out}} \times d_{\text{in}})}, \qquad \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial W}}_{(d_{\text{in}} \times d_{\text{out}})} = \underbrace{X^\top}_{(d_{\text{in}} \times B)} \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial Y}}_{(B \times d_{\text{out}})}$$
You never need to memorize matrix-calculus derivatives: $W^\top$ and $X^\top$ are the only ways to make the shapes match!

3. Rank, Outer Products, and Why LoRA Works

A matrix of shape $(4096 \times 4096)$ looks massive ($16{,}777{,}216$ numbers), but if every column is just a multiple of the first column, it only carries 1 independent direction of information. The number of linearly independent rows (or columns—they are always equal!) is the matrix's rank.

If you multiply a column vector $u \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times 1}$ by a row vector $v^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times d}$, you get a full $d \times d$ matrix called an outer product $u v^\top$ whose rank is $1$. More generally, multiplying a tall skinny matrix $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ by a wide flat matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d}$ creates a $(d \times d)$ matrix $\Delta W = BA$ with $\text{rank}(\Delta W) \le r$.

$$h = (W_0 + \Delta W)x = W_0 x + \underbrace{B}_{(d \times r)} \underbrace{(A x)}_{(r \times 1)}, \qquad \text{Parameters: } d^2 \;\longrightarrow\; 2dr$$
In LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation), $A$ compresses $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ down to an $r$-dimensional bottleneck, and $B$ expands it back to $\mathbb{R}^d$.

4. Eigenvectors, Eigenvalues, and SVD in Plain Geometry

When a square matrix $A$ multiplies a vector $x$, it usually both rotates and stretches $x$. An eigenvector $v$ is a special axis of $A$ that does not rotate at all—multiplying $v$ by $A$ simply scales it by a scalar eigenvalue $\lambda$:

$$A v = \lambda v \qquad (\text{direction of } v \text{ stays fixed; length scales by } \lambda)$$
For a symmetric matrix like a covariance matrix $\Sigma = \frac{1}{n}X^\top X$ or a loss Hessian $H$, the eigenvectors are always mutually orthogonal ($90^\circ$ apart).

You will never solve $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$ by hand in ML, because `torch.linalg.eigh` or `np.linalg.svd` does it in microseconds. What you do need is what eigenvalues and SVD mean in three places:

  • PCA (Principal Component Analysis): The eigenvectors of the feature covariance matrix $\frac{1}{n}X^\top X$ point along the orthogonal axes of greatest spread. Each eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ equals the variance along that axis.
  • Optimization & Loss Curvature (Hessian $H$): The largest eigenvalue $\lambda_{\max}$ of the Hessian matrix is the steepest curvature of the loss surface; gradient descent explodes if the learning rate exceeds $\eta > 2/\lambda_{\max}$.
  • SVD (Singular Value Decomposition, $X = U \Sigma V^\top$): Eigendecomposition only applies to square matrices. SVD generalizes it to any rectangular matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$, factoring $X$ into an input rotation $V^\top$, axis stretch factors $\Sigma = \text{diag}(\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \dots)$, and an output rotation $U$. Keeping only the top $r$ singular values gives the mathematically best rank-$r$ approximation of $X$.

5. Cheat Sheet: What to Keep vs. What to Ignore

Topic Verdict for ML Why
Shape tracking & transposes Essential daily 90% of PyTorch bugs are tensor shape mismatches in batched matrix multiplies or attention heads.
Dot products, cosine similarity, $L_1$/$L_2$ norms Essential daily Used in embeddings, retrieval, attention, weight decay, and gradient norm clipping.
Rank, low-rank $BA$, SVD & PCA High value Explains why LoRA, embedding compression, and PCA work.
Positive Semi-Definite (PSD) & eigenvalues $\ge 0$ Conceptual only Good to know that covariance matrices ($X^\top X$) have $\lambda_i \ge 0$ and Hessian $\lambda_{\max}$ sets max LR.
Hand determinants, Cramer's rule, cofactor inverses, manual RREF Skip completely Numerically unstable or $O(d^3)$; in code you solve $Ax=b$ with `linalg.solve` or optimize via SGD.
