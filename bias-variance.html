Overfitting, Underfitting, and the Bias-Variance Tradeoff

Every supervised learning model can fail in two opposite ways. If the model is too rigid—like trying to fit a straight line through a curved depreciation chart—it misses the underlying pattern right in front of it (underfitting, or high bias). If the model has too much freedom—like a high-degree polynomial or an unpruned decision tree that bends to hit every single training point—it memorizes random sample noise and falls apart the moment it sees new data (overfitting, or high variance).

Why does distinguishing those two failures matter before you try to fix a bad model? Because the cure for overfitting is poison for an underfit model, and vice versa. Spending two weeks collecting $10\times$ more training rows or cranking up regularization will rescue an overfit model, but will do nothing at all to help a high-bias model that is too simple to bend around the signal.

By comparing training error against validation error—and breaking expected test MSE into $\text{Bias}^2$ (aim error), $\text{Variance}$ (sample wobble), and irreducible noise $\sigma^2$—you can tell immediately which failure mode you are in and which lever will actually move the needle.

2. The Bias-Variance Tradeoff Equation (With Real Numbers)

Why can't we get zero bias and zero variance at the same time? Suppose at a fixed test input $x$, the true noise-free target is $f(x)$ and the observed test label is $y = f(x) + \epsilon$, where the random noise $\epsilon$ has mean $0$ and variance $\sigma^2$.

If we repeatedly draw fresh training datasets of size $N$ and train a fresh model $\hat{f}(x)$ each time, its expected Mean Squared Error on an unseen test point $(x, y)$ decomposes into three clean terms:

$$\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(y - \hat{f}(x))^2\big]}_{\text{Expected Test MSE}} = \underbrace{\big(\bar{f}(x) - f(x)\big)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2\text{ (Aim Error)}} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\hat{f}(x) - \bar{f}(x)\big)^2\Big]}_{\text{Variance (Sample Wobble)}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{Irreducible Noise}}$$
Click any symbol: $y$ = noisy test label, $f(x)$ = true signal, $\hat{f}(x)$ = prediction from one training sample, $\bar{f}(x) = \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)]$ = average prediction across training samples, $\sigma^2$ = label noise variance.
Optional 4-Line Proof: Why Do the Cross-Terms Cancel Out?

Add and subtract $f(x)$ and $\bar{f}(x)$ inside $y - \hat{f}(x)$:

1. Write $y - \hat{f}(x) = \underbrace{(y - f(x))}_{\epsilon} + \underbrace{(f(x) - \bar{f}(x))}_{-\text{Bias}} + \underbrace{(\bar{f}(x) - \hat{f}(x))}_{\text{Deviation from mean}}$.
2. Because test noise $\epsilon$ has mean $0$ and is independent of the training sample, all cross-terms with $\epsilon$ have expectation $0$, leaving $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^2] = \sigma^2$.
3. Because $(f(x) - \bar{f}(x))$ is a fixed constant and $\mathbb{E}[\bar{f}(x) - \hat{f}(x)] = \bar{f}(x) - \bar{f}(x) = 0$, the middle cross-term also vanishes!
4. That leaves the sum of the three squared pieces: $\text{Bias}^2 + \text{Variance} + \sigma^2$.

Worked numerical trace (3 models trained on 3 independent samples):

Fix a test car age $x = 5$, where the true noise-free price is $f(5) = 10$ ($\$10\text{k}$) and irreducible market noise is $\sigma^2 = 1$. We train a rigid linear model ($A$) and an unregularized deep tree ($B$) on $3$ separate training sets:

• Model A (Underfit Linear Model) predicts $\hat{f}_A = [6, \, 7, \, 8]$ across the 3 samples:
1. Average prediction: $\bar{f}_A$ $= \frac{6 + 7 + 8}{3} = \mathbf{7}$.
2. Squared Bias: $\text{Bias}^2$ $= (\bar{f}_A - f(5))^2 = (7 - 10)^2 = (-3)^2 = \mathbf{9}$ (consistently aims $\$3\text{k}$ too low).
3. Variance: Deviations from $\bar{f}_A = 7$ are $[-1, \, 0, \, +1]$, so $\text{Var}$ $= \frac{(-1)^2 + 0^2 + 1^2}{3} = \mathbf{\frac{2}{3}} \approx 0.67$ (very stable across samples).
4. Total Expected Test MSE: $9 + \frac{2}{3} + 1 = \mathbf{10.67}$.

• Model B (Overfit Deep Tree) predicts $\hat{f}_B = [6, \, 10, \, 14]$ across the 3 samples:
1. Average prediction: $\bar{f}_B$ $= \frac{6 + 10 + 14}{3} = \mathbf{10}$.
2. Squared Bias: $\text{Bias}^2$ $= (10 - 10)^2 = \mathbf{0}$ (on average, centered right on the bullseye!).
3. Variance: Deviations from $\bar{f}_B = 10$ are $[-4, \, 0, \, +4]$, so $\text{Var}$ $= \frac{(-4)^2 + 0^2 + 4^2}{3} = \mathbf{\frac{32}{3}} \approx 10.67$ (wild swings from sample to sample).
4. Total Expected Test MSE: $0 + \frac{32}{3} + 1 = \mathbf{11.67}$.

3. Hands-On Practice

Try It By Hand

Compute the Total Expected Test MSE for Model C

At test point $x = 5$, the true target is $f(5) = 10$ and irreducible noise is $\sigma^2 = 1$. A regularized tree (Model C) trained on $3$ datasets predicts $\hat{f}_C = [8, \, 9, \, 10]$:

Step 1 (Average prediction): $\bar{f}_C = \frac{8 + 9 + 10}{3} = 9$.
Step 2 (Squared Bias & Variance):
• $\text{Bias}^2 = (\bar{f}_C - f(5))^2 = (9 - 10)^2 = \mathbf{1}$.
• Deviations from $\bar{f}_C = 9$ are $[-1, \, 0, \, +1]$, and suppose across the full population of training sets its variance rounds cleanly to $\text{Var} = \mathbf{1}$.
Step 3 (Your turn — combine all three pieces): With $\text{Bias}^2 = 1$, $\text{Var} = 1$, and $\sigma^2 = 1$, what is the Total Expected Test MSE?

Notice how this decomposition immediately explains a classic ensemble mystery: why Random Forests (Bagging) are built out of deep, unpruned trees, whereas Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) is built out of shallow depth-4 stumps. Averaging $B$ independent models divides their variance by up to $B$ without changing their mean (bias)—so Random Forests start with deep, low-bias/high-variance trees and average away the wobble. By contrast, Boosting chains trees sequentially to fix earlier mistakes, which attacks bias—so it starts with shallow, high-bias/low-variance trees to keep variance from exploding.

4. Action Cheat Sheet: How Every Lever Moves Bias & Variance

Intervention / Knob Effect on $\text{Bias}^2$ Effect on Variance Use It When You See...
Increase L2 / L1 Penalty $\lambda$ (or Dropout) Rises slightly (shrinks weights toward $0$) Drops sharply (blocks wild weights) Overfitting (low train error, high val error)
Collect More Training Rows ($N \uparrow$) Roughly unchanged (cannot bend a straight line) Drops as $\sim 1/N$ (harder to memorize noise) Overfitting (wide gap between train & val)
Increase Model Capacity (depth, layers, interactions) Drops sharply (fits curved signals) Rises (more parameters to wobble) Underfitting (both train & val error are high)
Bagging / Random Forest (average $B$ deep trees) Unchanged (mean of trees = 1 tree's mean) Drops ($\text{Var}$ of average $\le$ single tree) Unstable deep decision trees with high variance
Boosting (add sequential residual trees) Drops sharply (corrects systematic errors) Rises if too many rounds without early stopping Shallow trees that underfit complex tabular patterns
